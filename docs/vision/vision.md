# Track Your Regions — Project Vision

## The Idea

Track Your Regions is a travel memory and discovery platform. It turns the question "where have you been?" into a rich, interactive experience — not just pins on a map, but a living record of your relationship with places around the world.

The core insight: travel is not binary. You don't just "visit" a place — you might pass through it on a train, explore its back streets for a week, or know everything about it from books without ever setting foot there. The app models this spectrum of connection and gives users tools to explore, track, and deepen their engagement with the world.

---

## User Roles

### Visitor (Not Authenticated)

A visitor can:

- **Browse the map** — see the world divided into regions (countries, sub-regions, custom groupings) rendered as interactive vector tiles
- **Explore experiences** — browse UNESCO World Heritage Sites, top museums, public art & monuments organized by region through the Discover page
- **See what exists** — understand the breadth of experiences available in any region, with counts, categories, images, and descriptions. A region is shown whole rather than as a first page of it: the map draws every experience assigned there, and the list reaches all of them — it answers about what the map is showing, with one line offering the rest of the region back. Neither is a first page, so a category is never represented only by its alphabetically-early members. Whole no longer means slow: a region holding hundreds of places used to lock the tab for a couple of seconds while it drew every row at once, and now draws the rows you are looking at as you scroll — the same list, the same scrollbar covering all of it, without the freeze. Opening a place's card no longer makes the list shuffle underneath you either. A card is not shown until everything in it is ready, so it opens once, at the size it keeps, and the rest of the list stays where you left it — where before it grew in steps and then jumped again when the picture arrived. While a card is being fetched the row you clicked says so quietly, without moving, and if your pointer rested on that row for a moment beforehand its picture is already waiting, which is the slowest part of the wait by far. An open card of a sprawling site now shows the first twenty of its places with a line offering the rest — the Historic Centre of Saint Petersburg has 112, and listing all of them at once was half a second of work before the card could appear. Running your pointer down that list is answered at once, on the row and on the map alike: it used to lag by three to five rows and light the map a second or two later, which made a card of many places feel stuck rather than slow. Closing a card leaves the map where you put it, too — it used to fly back out to the whole region, throwing away the view you had zoomed into and, with it, changing which rows the list was showing you
- **Switch world views** — see the world organized in different ways: any custom regional grouping an admin has published. Administrative geography is reachable this way only if an admin publishes a world view built from it (see the base layer import below); the built-in administrative hierarchy itself stays admin-only. The address bar follows the choice and the choice follows the address bar: a link carrying a world view opens on it, and pasting or editing one in a tab that is already open switches to it
- **Send someone a place** — a place has an address. The Historic Centre of Saint Petersburg is a link, and so is Europe, and so is the list of a country's World Heritage Sites in Discover: whoever opens it lands on that place, with the card already open on top of its region, rather than at the top of a continent with instructions to find it again. Refreshing the tab keeps you where you were, and Back walks the places you actually chose — the region you came from, the card you closed — rather than every twitch of the map. The link names the place in plain words (`/wv/5/r/6737-europe/e/1234-stonehenge`), and the browser tab and any preview of the link say what it is. What holds the link together is the object, not the wording: a site renamed by a correction or a curator keeps every link ever shared of it, and the words in the address quietly catch up. A link to something you are not allowed to see — a world view an admin has not published, a place that no longer exists — does not say so; it simply opens the nearest thing you can see, because a link should never become a way to find out what is being kept from you. Nothing about you goes into a link either: where you have been is yours, and addresses travel through other people's logs and chat windows
- **What just arrived** — a **New** mark on things you could first see recently, not on things that happen to be recent rows in a database. It stays for a while — how long depends on the source, since some publish monthly and some rarely — and then it fades on its own, per thing. It is not tied to a batch: a source that updates weekly shows several weeks' worth of new things at a time rather than only its latest run's, and a thing held back for review carries its mark from the day you could see it rather than from the day an importer found it
- **One answer to where something is** — a place you can actually go to. Some sites are one thing in a list and another on the map: the world's official register publishes a point for the *property*, which for a rainforest spread across a state can be 191 km from any part of it you could visit. What you are shown now is never that locator: it is a real place of the site. A site made of many parts is drawn as its parts, on the map and in Discover alike — the rainforest is its forty forests, the rock art is the shelters you can walk to — so what the map says is here is what is here. Where one point is unavoidable, it is the real place nearest to what the source published
- **The list beside the map follows it** — zoomed into a city, the list answers "what is here" rather than reading the whole region alphabetically: Europe offers 661 experiences, and a reader looking at Prague was given them from *Historic Centre of Prague* to *Wikipedia Monument* by initial. The rows are the ones the map is showing, each category says how many of its own are here — "12 of 467", the second number being the whole region's — and one line offers the rest of the region back, counting what the view is hiding. A card you have opened is the exception: it stays in the list wherever you then move the map, because a pan should not close what you are reading, and the list re-aims at it if the map's own flight moved it off screen. Zoomed out far enough that the whole region is on screen, the two questions have the same answer: every row is listed, the category counts show one number instead of two, and the line offering the rest of the region does not appear, because nothing is missing from the list. Discover's own list is unchanged: it is browsed by region rather than beside a map you are steering.
- **Fold a sprawling site back into one pin** — some sites are hundreds of places: the rock art of the Iberian Mediterranean is 734 shelters, the Roman frontier 420 forts and walls. Drawn in full they are the honest answer to "what is here", and they can also be more than you wanted while you look at everything else. Any one of them can be folded back to a single pin — from the map on either view, and from the row itself when you are browsing regions — and only that one: the rest of the region stays drawn as the places it is. A folded pin keeps a small count so it never reads as a place on its own, and unfolds with a click. The fold lasts as long as you are looking at that region — it is not a setting to remember or to find again
- **A site in trouble says so** — 58 of the world's heritage sites are inscribed on the List of World Heritage in Danger, and the card now says which, and since when: the Ancient City of Aleppo reads **In Danger since 2013**, the Ancient City of Tauric Chersonese since 2026. The date is the part that means something — a thirteen-year emergency is not the same news as this year's — and it is the source's own, not a guess. The mark had been in the product since the beginning and had appeared for nobody: the catalogue was reading the wrong half of what the official list sends, so every one of those 58 sites sat here looking like any other. A site the world takes back *off* that list loses the mark with it
- **Places that changed** — some sites leave the official lists without going anywhere. Those stay exactly where they were, on the map and in the lists, with a small **Former** mark and the reason: no longer listed, still there to visit. Places that genuinely no longer exist are a different matter — offering somewhere demolished as somewhere to go would be a lie, so they leave the lists, the map and the counts. On the map, a region that holds any says so at the end of its list, and one click brings them back for anyone curious what used to be there; Discover filters them the same way but has no way back yet
- **Only what a person has passed** — some sources are trusted outright; others, ones more prone to importer mistakes, are held back until a curator has looked. Once a source is gated, anything it wrote that nobody has checked yet is simply absent — no card, no marker, no line in a count — the same as if it did not exist. This applies at whatever size the gate catches a mistake: a whole new venue, one of its locations, or one work inside an otherwise-visible museum can each be held back on its own, without hiding the rest of what that source already offers. What being held back never does is take something away: when a gated source moves a place, the pin you have been seeing stays where it is until the new one has been checked, rather than the map going blank while a corrected coordinate waits for someone to look at it. So something that has ever been shown to you never falls off the map entirely while its source still offers it — an old pin left standing a little too long is the cost, and it is the one worth paying. Newly arrived points are a different matter and are simply not there yet, so a place that has just gained locations shows fewer of them than its source lists until a curator has looked. A region reaches a site through its points, so a point nobody has passed does not put its site into a region either: were a seventh temple of the Megalithic Temples of Malta to arrive in Ċentrali — the one Maltese region the other six are not in — that region would not list the site, count it, or draw a pin for it until somebody had read the temple. The three answers arrive together, on the day it is passed; a region never offers a site with nothing of it to show you
- **"New" means new to you** — the mark on a card says a visitor could first see this recently, not that an importer noticed it recently. Those are the same day for a trusted source and can be a week apart for a held one, where an object waits for a curator before anyone can see it at all. Anchoring the mark to the moment it became visible is what stops a held object arriving on your screen already unmarked, having spent its "new" while nobody could see it. Two consequences worth knowing. Marks now fade on their own schedule per object rather than all at once when a source next runs, so a weekly source shows several weeks' worth of new things at a time rather than only its latest batch. And everything that was already on the site before any of this wears no mark at all: nothing recorded when those things first became visible, so the honest answer is silence rather than a guessed date — the mark starts meaning something from the first thing published after the change. A mark is also spent only by a thing that actually reached your screen, not by a list that happened to contain it: opening a region of several hundred places used to count all of them as seen at once, so anything new below the fold quietly spent its "new" while you were still reading the top of the list

The visitor experience is designed to inspire curiosity and make the case for signing up — "look at all the things you could track."

### Authenticated User

Everything a visitor can do, plus:

- **Mark regions as visited** — click a region on the map to toggle it as visited, building a personalized travel map
- **Track experiences** — mark individual UNESCO sites, museums, and landmarks as visited with a simple checkbox. What you mark is yours to keep: no data refresh deletes it, whether the source renames what it calls a place or stops listing it altogether. A place the source stops offering leaves the lists, while your record of having been there is kept intact. One case is honest to name: when the source **replaces** a point rather than editing it — moves it more than ten metres, or files it under a new reference — that arrives as a new place, so a site you had finished shows as in progress again until you tick the new one. A coordinate merely written more precisely does not do this: within ten metres it is the same point, the same row, and your tick stays on it. Nothing is lost; the tick simply stays on the spot you actually visited
- **See progress** — visual feedback on how much of a region you've explored (experience completion counts). A place that no longer exists drops out of what a region counts as there to see, but never out of what you have seen: your visit to it stays, because you did go
- **Manage world views** — create and edit custom regional hierarchies with drag-and-drop region building, custom subdivision drawing, and AI-assisted boundary creation
- **Personal travel map** — a world map colored by your visited regions, visible at a glance
- **A New mark that waits for you** — the **New** window is the same for everyone, but signed in it is a floor rather than a deadline: arrive at the tail of it and the mark stays a week longer, counted from the first time it was actually shown to *you*. A batch is not something you can miss by visiting on the wrong day, and nothing a source does later takes the mark away — it runs out on its own

- **Change your password** — the **Account** page, reached from your own menu, shows who you are and lets you change the password you signed up with. It asks for the current one, refuses anything that has turned up in a public breach, and says the number of times it has, because that is what makes the refusal convincing rather than officious. Changing it signs out every *other* device and leaves the one you are on open — the page says so before you press, not after. An account that signs in with Google (or Apple) has no password here, so it is told where its password lives instead of being handed a form that cannot work

The authenticated experience is about reflection and tracking — turning scattered travel memories into a coherent, browsable record.

#### How to Sign Up

Two paths to an account:

- **Email/password** — register with a display name, email, and password. The app sends a verification email with a one-time link. Clicking the link verifies the email and logs you in. Until verified, login is blocked. The verification link expires after 24 hours; users can request a fresh one via "Resend verification email"
- **OAuth** — sign in with Google (or Apple). No email verification needed — the provider has already verified the address. Instant access

The registration form shows a "Check your email" confirmation state after submission, with the option to resend the verification email without leaving the dialog.

### Curator

Everything an authenticated user can do, plus content quality controls scoped to their assigned regions or sources:

- **Reject experiences** — hide incorrectly assigned or low-quality experiences from a region, with an optional reason. Rejections are region-scoped (rejecting from "Europe" doesn't affect "France")
- **Unreject experiences** — reverse a previous rejection if it was a mistake or circumstances changed
- **Edit experiences** — fix names, descriptions, categories, image URLs, and link URLs for any experience in their scope. An experience that sits in several regions counts as in scope when *any* of those regions is one the curator covers — the edit is on the experience, not on its place in one region. For a region-scoped curator the history entry is recorded under a region they cover, so it stays visible in their own view of that history; for a global or category curator, under no region at all, since their authority came from none in particular. The link URL points to a reference page (typically Wikipedia) and is shown as a clickable button in the experience details (next to the visited/curate actions). An image or link URL that is not a web address is refused as it is saved, rather than accepted and then quietly drawing nothing: a link has to be an ordinary `http(s)://…` address, and a picture that or a path to a file this site hosts. What the curator sees is still only the generic "Validation error" the API answers every rejected field with — the save is stopped, but the dialog does not yet say which field or why
- **Fix a place that is in the wrong place** — a curator can correct the name of one location inside an object, or move its point to where the thing actually is. Until now the only answers a held coordinate offered were to take the source's point or to keep it, which between them cannot fix a pin on the wrong building; and any correction made anywhere else was quietly written over the next time the source was asked. A corrected value now stays corrected: the source stops being able to overwrite that one field, while everything else about the point still follows it. Where the object is a single place, its own position moves with the point, so the list and the map agree rather than the map being right and the list a kilometre out. Where an object is made of many places — a serial site with forty components — only the component moves, because which of the forty the object as a whole should be pinned to is not something a single correction can answer. A moved point is reassigned to whatever region it now falls in, so correcting a coordinate across a border moves the place into the right country's list too. A correction can also be taken back, and it is taken back whole: where the source keeps proposing its own coordinates and a curator finally says the source was right, the pin moves back to the source's straight away and the object's own position follows at the next sync — handing back half of it would leave the object listed at one coordinate and drawn at another, which is the disagreement this exists to remove. Taking it back is only offered where the object is one place, though: on a serial site with forty components, a corrected component stays corrected, because the question "was the source right about this coordinate?" is asked about the object and there is no card that asks it about one of forty. **Today this is reachable through the API and not yet from a screen**: the place to offer it is the map a curator is already looking at when they can see the pin is wrong, and that map shows one point at a time only on the card that lists what a gated source is holding — which is what this same change makes list its rows. The correction is written down here because it is what the catalogue can now do; the control follows it.
- **Add existing experiences** — search the global database and manually assign an experience to their region when automatic spatial assignment missed it. Available via the Search & Add tab in the add dialog
- **Create new experiences** — add landmarks, venues, or points of interest that don't exist in any synced source. Each source group header in the experience list has a "+" button that opens the Create New dialog pre-set to that source (e.g. clicking "+" on "Top Art Museums" pre-selects the museum source). The curator must choose which source to file the experience under — there is no generic "Curator Picks" category. As soon as the curator types a name (3+ characters), the system automatically looks up the place and fills in coordinates, a Wikidata image, a short description, and a Wikipedia link. The lookup uses the current region name as implicit search context for better geo-disambiguation (e.g. typing "Holocaust Memorial" in the Berlin region searches "Holocaust Memorial Berlin"). This lookup fires only once — after it runs, the curator can freely edit the display name without losing the filled-in metadata. A suggestion info box shows the matched Wikidata entity with a "Re-lookup" link to explicitly re-search if needed. All fields remain fully editable, and a "Suggest" button provides manual image lookup as fallback. Location can also be set manually via a multi-mode picker: click on a map, search by place name, paste coordinates in any format (decimal, DMS, Google Maps URL), or describe the place in natural language for AI geocoding. A thumbnail preview shows below the image URL field. After creation, the dialog closes automatically and the new experience appears immediately in the list and on the map
- **Remove from region** — permanently remove a rejected experience from the region entirely (deletes the assignment). Unlike rejection (which hides), removal cleans the experience out of the region's list. The rejection record is kept as a guard — if a future spatial recompute re-adds the experience, it will automatically be hidden again. Available as an icon button in the rejected list (Map mode) and as a button in the curation dialog (Discover mode)
- **See rejected items** — rejected experiences appear dimmed with strikethrough in their list, invisible to regular users
- **Open an object nobody has checked yet** — a curator or admin whose scope reaches an experience can still open its page, its locations and its treasures directly by address, even while its source is gated and everyone else is shown nothing there. Every list, count, search and the map stay exactly as a visitor sees them either way — this only changes what happens at the object's own address, not what the catalogue is measured to hold
- **Work the queue as a list, not as a stack** — the review page is the questions on the left and the one under decision on the right. It was a single column of cards, and the cards themselves are why that stopped working: each now carries what its decision rests on — both versions of a disputed text in full, the object with its picture and its place, the works a rule counted — so nineteen of them stacked meant reaching the twentieth question by scrolling past nineteen answers' worth of evidence. A line in the list carries what a curator decides to *skip* by, which object and which category, and the rest waits on the right. Answering moves to the question that took its place rather than back to the top. Each kind of question pages on its own, and a heading claims "first 25" only where something really is behind it. Nothing on the screen recommends an answer: the two ways to settle a disagreement sit under the two versions they apply to, in the same order, and the marking that shows which words differ says the same thing about both columns rather than colouring one of them as the right one.
- **Answer what a sync run could not decide** — a review page collects the decisions a run leaves open, and nothing else. An object the source stopped listing gets one of three answers: it was delisted but is still standing, it no longer exists, or the source simply hiccupped and nothing happened. The first two are different facts about the same object and can both be true — a destroyed site can remain inscribed, an intact one can be struck off — so they are answered separately rather than as one status. A field where the source keeps proposing a value over a curator's edit shows both versions **in full, side by side, with the differing words marked** — no longer cut at a hundred and twenty characters, which is what made a two-hundred-character edit against five hundred characters of source text impossible to judge on the page that asked. **What a run proposes is a table of facts, and every fact speaks for itself.** A card says first what the run proposes — how many facts are new, how many changed, and which — and then lays them out one to a row: the fact's name, what readers see now, what the run proposes. The name is the fact's own, not the database's, and it carries its meaning: hover or tab onto *in danger* and the card says what the List of World Heritage in Danger is, what it means on the ground, and what a change to it usually means. A value looks the way readers see it — the In Danger badge is the badge, with the year the site was listed; the inscription criteria are the numerals with their meaning one hover away; a picture credit is the photographer and the terms; a coordinate is a coordinate; nothing is a dash. And under a value that changed, one line says what *this* change means to someone who has stood there: "Listed since 2003 — readers see no badge today; publishing adds it", "Moved 33 km north — may fall in a different region; check the pin", "Spelling only — the country is the same", "Inscription years do not change — check the source page". The source's extra data about an object used to arrive as the whole bag printed twice, side by side, for the curator to find the difference by eye — the whole of what a run proposed about the Bamiyan Valley, that its inscription criteria had arrived and a picture credit with them, was asked as eight named things on one side against six on the other. It is three rows now, and the two that arrive are marked as arriving. Two things a curator used to be asked about are not on the card at all: the machine's own labels, which nothing on the site reads and which repeated the criteria row beside them, and the storage the facts live in, which was never a fact. On a conflict card each fact is answered in its own column, and the two whole-card answers stay for the common case. **A change inside the object will read on the same card.** A run already records that a place of a serial site was renamed or a work's attribution corrected; when the card carries those, each part is its own group of rows under the object's, headed by the place's or the work's name with a way to open it — the table is built for that, and the object is only its first group. Around them the card now says what the decision rests on: the object itself, with its picture, its place on the map, the source's own page, and the regions it falls in; and per field, who wrote the version that stands, when the run that disagrees finished, and what anyone answered about that same field before — so the page stops calling another curator's work "my edit". Each field can be answered on its own, because a run improves and damages in the same breath, and taking a better description used to mean taking a mangled name with it; **Both answers are answers now.** Accepting the source's value lifts the protection on that field, which is what applies it and takes the question off the page. Keeping the curator's own used to be the absence of an action: visitors went on seeing their version, and the identical card came back after every single run — one object's arrived three times in two days with the source proposing the same text each time. Now it is a button, and it settles the question the same way accepting does. What it settles is narrow on purpose: the answer is about *that text*, so a source that later proposes something different is heard again, while one that keeps repeating itself is not. The two answers are not mirror images about what visitors see, and it is worth being exact: accepting puts the source's text on the site — right away for most fields, at the next sync for a moved location or a metadata key — while refusing changes nothing anyone can see, because the curator's version was already the one on display. So refusing is only ever a decision about whether the disagreement stays open. A curator can leave the card alone as before, and the page no longer pretends that is the only way to stand by their own words. A few kinds of disagreement — a moved location, a metadata key — cannot be applied on the spot; accepting those lifts the curator's protection and the next sync brings the value in, which the page says plainly rather than leaving the curator to guess when it takes effect. There is one exception, and the page says so instead of hiding it: when a category's own rule turns a row down — an archaeological museum in a list of art museums, a stretch of painted wall — the row is already hidden from visitors, because a candidate that fails the same rule is never added in the first place and one that predates the rule has to end up in the same place. That is not a guess about the world, so it is not offered as one: the card states the rule's own objection and asks a different question — was the rule right? **The objection is stated in ordinary words, and the evidence behind it can be looked at.** What a rule records for itself — "painting-share: 2 painting(s) vs 9 sculptural work(s)" — names internal tests, states no threshold and never says where its numbers came from; read once it is opaque, and a curator reads nineteen of them at a sitting. So the card says what was found in one sentence, a question mark beside it holds how the rule decides and how much it takes to pass, and the number in the sentence is the thing to hover: it opens the works themselves, with their pictures, their makers and their dates. "None of the 5 famous works is a painting" is a claim a curator cannot check; five named sculptures they can see is a question they can answer. Where the sentence names a single work instead of counting — the painting that pulled a cathedral into a list of museums — that work is shown first and whatever else the place holds is listed under it, so a phrase naming one thing never opens an unlabelled list of three. Either answer sticks against the machine, and no later run reverses it. Keeping a row out does not stick against the person who did it: because a kept-out row is hidden from every list and gives nothing back at its own address, the page keeps its own kept-out list, collapsed at the foot, where one click puts any of them back. Apart from that, until someone answers, none of it has changed anything visitors see
- **See what a gated source is holding back** — three more kinds of open question surface on the same review page, for a source held back until a person looks (see "Only what a person has passed" above). An **arrival** is a whole new object nobody has passed yet — a museum, a site, an artwork — surfaced here with everything on it, because a curator's own screen is where the gate is allowed to open even before anyone has answered for the object. A **held proposal** is the opposite shape: an object visitors already see, where the source wants to change something about it and the change is being kept off that screen until someone looks. It is not lost — the version visitors see stays exactly as it is. The third kind is what a visible object has quietly gained: a museum holding newly-arrived paintings, a site newly-arrived locations. The card gives both the number and the things themselves — the points by name with their coordinates, the works by title and maker — capped at a page's worth, because nobody reads seven hundred rows and nobody can answer for twelve works they cannot see either. All three sit under one heading — "Waiting to be published" — because they share one sentence: none of it has reached a visitor. **An object is asked about once, however many of the three are true of it.** A museum whose label is being held back *and* which gained twelve paintings is one card with one heading and a row for each thing that is open — the label with both versions side by side, the new points listed under their count, the new works under theirs — because that is one object and one decision to the person looking at it, whatever it took to work that out. The one question that stays separate is a disagreement about the curator's own wording: that is already decided, by them, and keeps its own card, so the same museum can appear twice with two different questions rather than once with two answers merged into a button. And a curator can look at the thing itself before deciding — one click on the card opens what a gated object actually holds, which is the only place an unpublished arrival can be seen at all, since it appears in no list, no count and on no map
- **Answer half a question when only half of it is doubtful** — a museum can be waiting on two things at once: a sentence its source wants to rewrite, and twelve paintings that arrived since anyone looked. Those used to be one act, so a curator who did not want the new sentence kept the paintings invisible by refusing it — one doubtful field freezing twelve checked works. The card now offers the change on its own, and the unread points and works stay exactly where they were, waiting for their own answer. And the things waiting are listed rather than counted: the points by name with their coordinates, the works by title and maker with the most famous first, so a decision about twelve paintings is made by looking at twelve paintings. Where there are more than a page of them — a serial site can hold hundreds — the card shows the first twenty-five and says plainly how many there are, rather than letting a short list stand for a long one.
- **Say that visitors may see it** — the answer to all three of those, and the only way anything a gated source wrote reaches a visitor. Publishing an arrival takes the whole object: it appears in lists, on the map and in counts, and the points and works that arrived with it appear with it, because nobody wants a museum published with its paintings still held back. Publishing a held proposal applies what the source wanted to change and takes the question off the page. And a visible object holding newly-arrived contents can have those published on their own, without re-answering for the object — three checked paintings do not make the museum re-read. A curator can publish without settling a separate disagreement about their own edit: whether visitors may see this and whose wording it is are different questions, so a field the curator has claimed keeps their version and the rest of the object still goes out. **That is also how to take everything but one thing**: a curator who wants the twelve new paintings and not the new label writes the label their own way first — editing a field claims it — and then publishes; their wording stays, everything else lands, and the page says which fields it left alone. The card says so where the proposed wording is shown, because an escape route nobody is told about is not one. What a curator was looking at is checked against what is stored before anything is written, so a card drawn before another run landed is refused with what the object says now rather than publishing a proposal nobody read. One thing publishing will not do is settle two questions at once: an object its own category turned down cannot be published until that refusal is answered, and the page says so rather than quietly making it visible — putting it back is what publishes it, in one step, so nobody is asked the same thing twice. That step only does something if nobody had looked at the object yet: for one visitors could already see before its category refused it, putting it back is just the admission answer, since the refusal was the only thing that had hidden it. Publishing an already-visible object is harmless and repeatable — it changes nothing that was already decided, which is what makes it the way to take newly-arrived contents under a row that visitors can already see. Publishing a point that replaces another does one thing more, and it is the point of the click: the pin it replaces stops being shown at the same moment, so nobody ever sees the same place twice, and the regions the object counts towards are recomputed for the point that is now the real one. **Every publication says what it did**, in one line above the queue: which wording landed, how many points and works became visible, and — the two nothing else would ever record — the moment a replaced pin stopped being shown, and whether recomputing the object's regions failed afterward, so a publication that landed with stale regions is never read as an unqualified success. **That last one names the world views it left stale**, because rebuilding them is an admin's job and not the curator's: the one thing a curator can do is tell an admin which object and which world view, so the line says exactly that instead of leaving them to report that something about regions went wrong somewhere. "Put it back" on a refusal says the same way whether the row is now visible or whether it only answered the refusal, rather than leaving a curator looking for a second button. And a card answered by someone else in the meantime says what the server now holds and stays live rather than going dead: the page redraws itself against the newer state instead of leaving a button that can only fail
- **Answer for one place inside an object** — an object is often made of several places: a serial World Heritage site of seven housing estates, a museum with one address and two hundred works. When a run stops offering *one of those places*, visitors lose that pin straight away — sometimes one they had ticked — and until now nobody was asked about it and no screen said it had happened. The review page now carries that question too, one row per object and one answer per place, and it is placed above the refusals for that reason: a refusal never showed the row at all, while this has already taken something away. Every card says what the object is made of, which is the piece that was missing everywhere: a proposed description of one estate of seven used to sit against the whole site's text with nothing to say the site had parts. And each lost place carries the two facts that make the answer decidable rather than a guess — whether anyone had been there, and how far away the source now lists that same part. The distance matters more than it sounds: the first real case in the catalogue turned out to be **one centimetre**, a coordinate rewritten at finer precision, and a page that called that "moved" would have been wrong about the only thing it was for. A run no longer asks that question about a rewrite: ten metres or less is read as the same place written more exactly, and no card is raised for it. A few such questions are still reachable by other routes, and the card says so itself rather than pretending otherwise. The ones already recorded against a place somebody had visited are left exactly as they are, because moving a person's record of having been somewhere is a decision for a curator and not for a repair script, so those still come round to be answered. And where a source publishes two parts of one component a stone's throw apart — nothing in the catalogue does, and it would be the source disagreeing with itself about what one part is — the run can still ask once about a part that never went anywhere. Ten metres is chosen to be smaller than the things being pointed at, so the opposite still reaches a curator: a source that re-centres a national park by two kilometres, or moves a museum's pin across the square to a new entrance, has made a judgement about a place, and that is theirs to confirm. The three answers are the same three an object gets — the source dropped it, it no longer exists, or the source was wrong — and two of them change nothing anyone sees, because the place was already off the map the moment the run noticed. Only "false alarm" puts it back. Nothing is ever deleted: whoever had been there keeps that record whatever is answered
- **Take a verdict back** — a mis-click is one click away from hiding an object from everyone, so it has to be one click away from coming back. Open the item and it offers exactly that: "It is still listed" for something recorded as delisted, right where the item already sits — those are never hidden — and "It does still exist" for something recorded as gone, which first has to be revealed, since hiding it is what the verdict did. For those two the review queue cannot host it — the queue lists open questions, and an answered one has left it — which is why it lives where the object is still reachable. Two things have nowhere else to live, and the queue keeps both after all, out of the way of the work but one click from coming back: a row a rule kept out, and a **place an object lost that somebody has already answered about**. A place is not something you can open on its own — it is a pin on a map, and both answers take the pin off the map — so once the question is answered it is on no screen at all, and the answer could not be undone. Now it is listed: what was decided, when, by whom, and the note they left. Taking an answer back makes the person taking it the author of where the place now stands, so the note goes with the answer it explained rather than being reattributed to somebody who never wrote it — and the object's own History is where the wording stays readable, which is a promise the History had to be taught to keep: a verdict on a place used to appear there as a machine word with nothing under it. Each answer is taken back on its own, because they are two different claims — that the source stopped listing the place, and that the place is gone from the world — and the pin comes back only once neither is still holding it, which the screen says before the click rather than after. What it will not pretend is that taking an answer back settles anything with the source: if the source still does not offer the place, the next run will notice and ask again
- **View curation history** — every action (edit, reject, unreject, create, assign, remove, accepting a source value, refusing one, publishing, and the lifecycle verdicts above) is logged with who did what and when, viewable per experience. Each entry says what the act was in the words the product uses for it — “kept out”, “put back”, “took the source’s” — rather than the machine's name for the column, and shows what the row holds that the name cannot: the fields a publication applied and the ones it left as the curator wrote them, the value taken from the source and the one waiting for the next sync, the pin handed back with a coordinate, the picture credit dropped with a picture. **A note a curator typed is on that screen at last.** Two cards ask for one — the object a run stopped finding, and the row a rule kept out — and each says on the field that it will be kept in this object's curation history; it was kept and shown nowhere, so the sentence explaining why something was marked gone, or why a refusal was overruled, existed only in the database. Refusing is in that list for a reason worth stating: standing by your own words used to leave no trace at all, so the history could show a field accepted from the source three times and say nothing about the times someone declined it. The history a region-scoped curator sees is bounded by that scope: an experience assigned to several regions shows only the entries for the regions they cover, plus entries that name no region. Global and category curators, and admins, see the whole history

Curators are the quality layer. They're local experts or subject-matter specialists who keep the content accurate and relevant. The system trusts them — nobody approves a curator's work, and there are no drafts. The review page runs the other way round: it is the machine asking a curator, not a curator waiting on anyone. Actions are logged for accountability, and curator edits are protected from being overwritten by automated syncs.

### Admin

Everything a curator can do (implicitly, without needing explicit curator assignments), plus infrastructure management:

- **Decide which sources need a person's eye** — one switch per source, reading "Hold new and changed content for review", with the consequence written under it rather than left to be discovered. Held means new objects from that source arrive invisible to visitors and a change to an object they already see is kept back, until a curator passes it. What the switch never does is take something away: everything published before it was flipped stays exactly as visible as it was, so one source can hold newly-held objects beside long-published ones. Turning it off is equally quiet — the click itself publishes nothing, because a switch that made forty unreviewed objects visible in one go is exactly what holding them back exists to prevent. What becomes of a backlog afterward is two answers rather than one, and the panel gives both beside the switch in either of its positions — while the gate is on as what the click would do, once it is off as where things now stand, and as kinds without numbers where the counting failed. Objects nobody has read, and unread points or works, keep waiting until a person releases them, while a change that was being held back is applied by that source's next run — holding it back was the gate's doing, and the gate is gone. The panel also says, per source, what is waiting and in what shape: objects nobody has read, objects visitors can see that are holding a change, objects visitors can see that are holding unread points or works. Three sentences rather than one number, because "eighteen waiting" means something different for eighteen unread museums than for eighteen paintings inside one published museum. And where the counting itself fails, the panel says *that* — "how much this source is holding could not be counted" — and offers no release for that source until it can name what the release would publish. "Nothing waiting" is the sentence an admin acts on by leaving a held source alone, so it is not a sentence to print when nobody counted; the sources, and everything needed to run or stop a sync, are still there either way. What the switch still says in that state is both answers **as kinds rather than counts** — unread things would wait for a person, a change being held back would be applied by the next run — because how much is unknown while what-becomes-of-it is not, and that second half is the only consequence here a flip back cannot undo: once a run has applied a held change, re-holding the source restores nothing
- **Run syncs** — trigger and monitor data synchronization from external sources (UNESCO, Wikidata for museums, landmarks). A **Dry run** answers "what would this change?" without changing anything: it fetches from the source, works out the difference against what is stored, and records the result as a preview that writes no experiences. The same run can be previewed as often as needed before it is spent. **A sync cannot destroy anything a person recorded.** There is one kind of run, and it refreshes what the source still offers, adds what is new, and marks what the source stopped offering — visits, the individual points people ticked off, the artworks they logged as seen, curator edits and manual region assignments all survive it. The old **Force sync**, which emptied the source's whole catalogue and every visit record with it before starting again, is gone: with nothing left to delete it was a mode that differed from an ordinary run in nothing but the damage
- **Release everything one source is holding, in one act** — for a source being turned off, or one whose backlog was never the point, there is a single "Publish all waiting" beside it rather than a queue to walk. It says what it will do before it does it, and says it per kind rather than as one number: how many objects nobody has read become visible to visitors, and — separately — how many objects visitors can already see gain the points and works waiting under them without themselves “becoming visible”, because they are visible already. Each is recorded on its own, so the history still says when a museum became visible, and what was released under one visitors already had. It also says what it will **not** do — a change to an object visitors can already see is never published in a batch, because publishing it rewrites what they are looking at with a value nobody read, and that belongs on the card that shows the old wording beside the new one. So those stay waiting on purpose, the notice says how many — or says outright that it could not count them, where the count itself failed after the publications had landed, because a zero there would be a claim about the source nobody checked. **Nothing a single publication would have told them is dropped because there are forty of them**: how many points and works became visible, how many replaced pins stopped being shown and in which objects, and which objects landed with their regions not recomputed, named with the world views an admin has to rebuild. Read off a batch of forty, each of them is otherwise recoverable only by opening forty histories, which is the work this button exists to avoid. A curator whose scope is a set of regions releases what they cover and is told how many were outside it, instead of reading a short result as a cleared source
- **See what a sync actually did** — a run opens into a per-object report rather than four totals: which objects were created, which fields changed on which object (and whether the change matters — a site entering the danger list is not a reworded description), which came through untouched, which the source stopped listing, where the source now disagrees with a curator's edit, and which entries the source offered that this category does not hold — a catalogue's collection is not a museum, and setting it aside is not a failure. **An object whose *contents* moved is one of those changes, and reads as its own kind.** A run that added a place to a serial site, or hung a work in a museum, used to leave nothing in the report at all: every field of the object had come through, so there was no row. Now there is one, marked **contents** rather than updated — not counted among the run's updates, because the object did not change, the museum did — and it names what arrived rather than counting it: "places: gained Waldsiedlung Zehlendorf". The report defaults to the changes worth a look, and what it hides is a minor field edit that moved nothing else: a row whose contents moved stays even when the only edited field was a reworded description, because that row is the only record anywhere that the component arrived, and a row where the source ran into a curator's own edit stays too, however small the field, because a run's one disagreement with a person is the row an admin opens the report to find. A change a gated source proposed and the gate kept back reads as exactly that, and never as a change that landed: the object is marked as **held** rather than updated, it is not counted among the run's updates, and the field says which value visitors are still seeing and which one is waiting for someone to look. That is a different mark from a field where the run ran into a curator's own edit, because the two say opposite things about who is waiting — one has already been decided, the other has not been looked at at all. **A number the import keeps about its own run is not a change.** How many works a pass hung in a museum, and how famous those works add up to being, are the catalogue's own bookkeeping — and the second moves whenever anybody anywhere adds a language link to any painting a museum holds. Behind a gate, every one of those moves became a question: a card asked a person to approve the Louvre's fame total going from 2363 to 2365, and the next run asked again, for nearly every museum, with no end to it. Worse, the answer never arrived, so the number a person was being asked about was also the number that never got written: the stored total stayed at what it read the day the gate went up, while every run since worked out a different one. Nobody saw a wrong figure — that number is shown on no screen today, and the order museums come out in is worked out fresh each run rather than read back from it. What there was, was a question nobody could answer and a record that had quietly stopped following the thing it records. Those numbers now go straight into the row, and appear in no report and on no card. What was counted is still reported exactly as before: a painting arriving in a museum is a thing that happened to the museum, and that is the part somebody can actually answer for. **And the run's own summary says how many of its changes were held back.** A run from a gated source used to report itself as one that touched nothing — "created 0, updated 0, unchanged 1272" — while each of those 1272 was a change to a visible object that a curator had yet to answer, visible only from the curator's queue, which the admin reading the run list may not be the person watching. The run list and the run's card now carry that number beside the others, and it climbs while a run is still going, so "the source said nothing new" and "the source proposed a change a person must answer" stop reading the same. It is the count of held *changes*, and only that: a whole new object is already in the Created figure, and a new point or work arriving inside an object visitors can see is neither — those two are counted per source rather than per run, on the gate's own panel, which says all three kinds side by side
- **Region assignment is not a step to remember** — a sync places whatever moved during the run, so an admin no longer has to notice that new objects arrived and press anything. **What a run remembers, and how to make it forget.** A source's runs keep what the outside world told them — the class trees, the pools of famous works, where each work says it hangs — so that a collection interrupted half-way resumes instead of starting over, and so a question whose answer has not changed is not asked again. None of it is ever shown to a visitor: it only decides whether a sync asks again. Each source's card carries its own cache, closed until an admin opens it, listing what is in it, how old the oldest part is, when the next part expires, and how long each part lives by rule — with the reason for that rule in the same sentence, because "an ontology moves over months" is what makes a week a defensible number. The lifetime can be changed per part, and the change applies to what is already there: an answer keeps its own age, so shortening the rule can expire it at once, and the panel says how many were re-dated. Any part can be cleared, which means "ask again". And a single run can ignore the whole cache without deleting it. Only the sources that actually keep something show any of this — a card that offered to clear a cache the source never fills would be inventing an explanation.

- **The photographer is named.** Almost every picture in the catalogue was taken by somebody and is served from somebody else's machine — UNESCO's, or Wikimedia Commons' — and most carry a licence that asks one thing of anyone showing it: that the person who took it is credited wherever it appears. For a long time the catalogue credited nobody. Now a quiet line under each picture says who took it and under what terms, linked to where those terms are stated: *Stefan Kühn · CC BY-SA 3.0* under Stonehenge, *Benoît Prieur · CC0* under the Little Mermaid. It is deliberately small — it is owed to the photographer, not offered to the reader — and where a source names nobody, nothing is shown rather than an empty line implying nobody took it. **A credit never outlives its picture either**: where the photograph does not arrive — and on some sources most of them do not — the line goes with it, rather than naming a person beside an empty frame. **A curator who puts a different picture on an object takes the old credit off with it**: the line either names whoever took the new one, or says nothing. What it never does is leave one person's name under another person's photograph. **The works inside a museum are credited on the same terms as the museum itself** — the photographs of paintings, statues and steles that the catalogue shows and does not own. Most of them are of paintings old enough to be free of copyright, and there the file names the painter, whom the row already names; a line repeating the artist and adding "Public domain" is not a credit, it is noise that teaches a reader to stop looking at the place a real credit will appear. So on a list the line draws where it carries something: a licence that asks to be honoured — the Mesha Stele in the Louvre was photographed by Mbzt under CC BY-SA 4.0 — or a photographer who is not the artist. Rest on a work and the picture fills the map at a size worth looking at, with the whole credit under it. **The screens curators work on name the photographer too** — the review queue's cards and the picture they enlarge, the preview of what is waiting to be published, the search results when an object is being added. Working on the catalogue rather than publishing it does not change whose photograph it is. And a curator who creates an object by hand has its picture credited from the moment they save it, rather than waiting for an import that will never look at a row a person wrote.

- **What you have seen can be ticked off without a mouse.** The two lists a visitor records against — the places a serial site is made of, and the works a museum holds — are where you say what you have actually stood in front of, and until now both took a pointer: someone reading with a keyboard could go through every work in the Louvre, or every one of a heritage site's parts, and record none of them. Neither list shows itself whole, so the way in mattered as much as the rows, and the two lists hide themselves differently. On the object's own page both are folded shut past fifteen entries: the heading that opens each is now a control the keyboard reaches, and it says whether what you are about to open is already open. Beside the map the works list has no fold at all — it shows ten and keeps the rest behind a single "show all", which was likewise a thing only a mouse could press, so a reader could tick off ten of the Louvre's holdings and reach none of the other hundred. Inside, each row is a stop the keyboard reaches, and what answers depends on what the control is: the tick box beside a place or a work takes Space, while the tiles and the headings, being buttons, take Space or Enter. What a screen reader announces is the thing itself and what the press will *do* with it: "Ħaġar Qim — mark as visited", "The Night Watch — mark as seen", and the opposite once it is. Before, the tick boxes had no names at all, so a reader heard "checkbox, not checked" and had to guess which of thirty places it belonged to. Where the keyboard is is visible, too — whatever it has reached is outlined, which on a grid of near-identical squares is the difference between navigating and guessing, and which every control in the product gained at once rather than these two alone. **And a visitor who is not signed in is no longer offered the control at all** — they keep the picture, its title and its credit, where before they met a tile that invited a click and answered it with silence, because recording anything needs an account to record it against.

**Being a good guest of the sources we import from.** Every source is somebody else's server, and the rules are theirs. The World Heritage list now arrives in a single request rather than thirteen paged ones — the portal allows ten thousand calls a day and publishes what is left of them, which the run reads and records — and asking it for only the fields the catalogue actually uses cut the answer from 24 MB to under 4. That last change also ended a long silence. The importer had been asking for an inscription-criteria field that does not exist in that dataset, so **not one** of the 1,272 heritage sites carried its criteria; previewing the fix turned up two more of the same kind, and the catalogue was recording **none** of the 58 sites the world lists as in danger and **none** of the 51 that cross a border. All three are read correctly now, and where an admin has put that source behind the curation gate, the missing years arrive as one batch of proposals to approve rather than as a silent rewrite. The 58 sites in danger were the exception and were simply put right: a curator's job is to judge what a source says, and being asked 58 times to approve the correction of our own misreading is not that job — nobody would have seen the mark until they had finished, either. All of it — the heritage portal and the two Wikidata imports alike — now waits out a bad day at the source instead of ending the run on the first refusal, and shares one quarter-hour of patience across a phase of a run rather than granting it per question — the collecting half and the crediting half each get one, so neither can spend the other's.

**A run that is waiting says it is waiting.** Before the first object is written there is nothing to count — nobody knows how many museums the source will offer until it answers — so the panel shows a moving indeterminate bar and the phase it is in, rather than a percentage parked at zero, which reads as a run that has hung. And when the source itself is having a bad day, the panel says so in those words: which error it gave, that we are retrying, how long until the next attempt, and how much of the waiting is spent. That is the difference between an admin watching a stuck screen and an admin deciding to come back in ten minutes — a real museum run failed exactly there, after a minute of invisible retries. The run says the rest while it happens too: after the last object it reports **assigning regions** rather than finishing, for as long as that takes — minutes on a category's first run — and Cancel is unavailable from the moment the last object is processed — through the tidying up and the assigning that follow — because by then there is nothing left for a stop to interrupt, and pretending otherwise would report a cancellation that never happened. **Cancel is answered while the run is waiting, too.** A collection that is riding out a bad quarter-hour at the source can be minutes from its next question, and a stop that only took effect when the waiting ended was, from the admin's side, a button that did nothing: the wait now ends on the press, and the question already in flight is dropped rather than finished for nobody. When a press genuinely lands on nothing — the run had already ended, or the server was restarted under it — the card says which of those it was, instead of leaving the admin to press again harder. There is no standing "assignment needed" warning any more, because a run that succeeds leaves no such question open. The Region Assignment panel remains for two cases a sync cannot settle by itself. The ordinary one is that **region boundaries changed**, so every location has to be tested against them again. The other is that **the placement at the end of a run failed** — the run then shows as Partial rather than Success, with the reason in its log entry, because the catalogue is correct while the region assignments for what it moved are not. That is the one situation where an admin does have to press the button. It rebuilds a whole world view and clears its automatic assignments first, so regions read as empty while it runs — which is exactly why a sync does not use it
- **Manage world views** — full CRUD on regional hierarchies, assign divisions to regions, compute geometries. When splitting GADM divisions into children, existing child regions (e.g., from a WorldView import) appear as assignment targets — admins manually assign which GADM geometry goes to which existing region via dropdowns. The "Create Custom Subregions" dialog also pre-populates target groups from existing child regions, letting admins drag divisions into imported regions. The Map View tab highlights the image overlay button when a region map is available — one click loads the map from Wikimedia Commons as a reference image, viewable either as a map overlay or side-by-side with the division map for precise geometry alignment. The dialog can be opened for any region — when a region has child regions but no direct divisions, descendant geometries are shown as a read-only context layer (color-coded by group) while only direct members remain interactive. A region shown by its territorial extent rather than its coastline follows the box that says so: ticking “use hull” now redraws the region on the main map at once, where before the overview kept the outline traced from its real coastline while the island layer switched to the hull immediately — one region drawn as two different shapes, the stale one served from cache on top of it. Every member is drawn, however small: the outlines are simplified for the screen in a way that cannot delete one, where a division under about half a kilometre across used to be simply absent from the map its region was being edited on — 3,366 of GADM's divisions are that small, Rwandan cells and Philippine barangays among them. A "Move to parent" tool lets admins click individual divisions to push them up to the parent region, and an "All to parent" batch action moves all unassigned divisions at once. Several search results can be staged and made one region in a single step; the dialog suggests the name the staged names share — "Kazakhstan" for the two rows GADM keeps for it — and then leaves the field to the admin: the suggestion can be replaced or cleared, and a cleared field stays cleared. **Manual Cluster Paint Editor** — when the CV auto-match pipeline produces incorrect cluster boundaries, admins can switch to a vector-border paint editor with paint bucket (flood fill), border eraser, and polyline drawing tools to manually correct cluster assignments. Borders are extracted from the cluster output as SVG vector paths (via OpenCV findContours) and rendered as a scalable overlay with smooth curves; the eraser splits paths at the cursor, and the line tool draws new polylines with automatic endpoint snapping. Flood fill rasterizes the SVG on demand and stops at the vector borders. The editor overlays a transparent color canvas on the map and submits the result to replace automated pixel labels before ICP alignment proceeds
- **Describe a world view without hitting a wall** — every description field — the create dialog, the settings dialog, and the inline editor in the world view header — carries a live character counter and stops at 1,000 characters, the length the system keeps — so the limit shows up while the description is being typed, rather than as a save that fails for no stated reason
- **Publish or hide world views** — toggle "Visible to everyone" per world view in its settings dialog (custom world views only — the picker offers no Settings entry for the default world view, so its flag is reachable only through the API); a hidden one is unlisted and unreadable through the API to everyone else (its map tiles aren't yet scoped by the same visibility check), and newly imported world views start hidden until an admin publishes them.
- **Import WorldView** — one panel with a source selector: Wikivoyage, a JSON file, or the administrative base layer. Choosing **Wikivoyage** and clicking "Fetch from Wikivoyage" automatically extracts the full Wikivoyage region hierarchy (~4,500 regions), enriches with Wikidata IDs, creates a WorldView, and matches countries to administrative divisions — all from a single button click with live multi-phase progress (extraction → enrichment → import → matching, 20-40 min). Choosing **JSON file** uploads a pre-generated JSON tree for any other external source. A region's map image in that tree is held to what a picture may be — an absolute http(s) address, refused at upload otherwise — and is drawn only from a host the app trusts for pictures (Wikimedia Commons, where every map imported so far lives), so a map hosted elsewhere is kept but never shown, and the picker offers only maps it can draw. A region's source page — the Wikivoyage article it came from — is held to the same shape at upload, and is offered as a link (the glyph in the review tree, the source-page button in the Custom Subregions map view, "View source page" in the CV match review) only where it is an http(s) address; anything else is named, not linked. Choosing the **administrative base layer** imports the administrative divisions currently loaded into their own world view — one region per division, down to a chosen depth — matched by the same pipeline and landed in the same review as any other import; like any new import, the world view starts hidden. The Wikivoyage fetch uses a persistent cache — admins pick a named cache snapshot from a dropdown (showing size + age per snapshot), choose "Clean fetch (no cache)" to force a fresh fetch, or delete unwanted snapshots. Each successful run saves a timestamped snapshot for future re-use. Each source gets a distinct source type (`wikivoyage`, `imported`, or `base_layer`) shown in the existing world views list. Matching policy follows the source rather than being asked for: Wikivoyage and JSON-file imports get "Country-based", which looks for country names anywhere in the tree, and the administrative base layer gets "Hierarchical", which walks down the division tree alongside the imported one — that is what lets it resolve every region of a mirror instead of the 62% the country-based route reached. A JSON-file import can also be set to "None" to skip auto-matching entirely and assign everything by hand. Review/accept matches through a dedicated admin interface with a hierarchical tree view. The tree view uses role-based rendering: containers show "X/Y matched" summaries, countries show status chips with GADM names, and `children_matched` countries expand to show subdivision assignments. Sub-continental groupings (Melanesia, Polynesia, etc.) can be handled via a "Handle as sub-continental" button that clears the parent's match and re-runs country-level matching on each child independently. Division assignments made via the WorldView Editor are automatically reflected back in the match review interface (bidirectional sync). A "Re-match All" button resets and re-runs the matcher after improvements, under the same policy the source implies — the dialog is a plain confirmation and offers no policy choice — it deletes every division assignment for the world view first, including ones accepted by hand, so hand-work belongs after a re-match rather than before it. Because a run costs 20 seconds to two minutes and each one discards the previous result, the button is limited to five presses a minute and answers "too many requests" beyond that. "Close Review" finalizes the import (disabled until every region is matched and the coverage check reports no active uncovered administrative divisions). **Simplify Hierarchy** — collapse fully-covered subtrees (where every GADM child of a parent is already a member) up into the parent region with one click, useful when GADM has more granularity than the world view needs. **Smart Simplify** — detects when a GADM parent's children are split across multiple sibling regions and proposes consolidating them into one, showing a side-by-side map view of current vs proposed assignments so the admin can apply or skip each move. Spatial anomalies (exclaves and disconnected fragments) are highlighted in the same dialog with Accept/Skip actions. **Geoshape Match** — fetches the Wikidata geoshape for the Wikivoyage region and uses IoU scoring to find the best-covering set of GADM divisions; if no geoshape exists, automatically builds a composite from child entities (via Wikidata SPARQL + Wikivoyage regionlist). **Point Match** — for regions without any geoshape, extracts Wikivoyage marker coordinates and finds GADM divisions that contain those points; marker locations are stored for preview in the Division Preview Dialog as orange circle markers on the left map panel. The Division Preview Dialog adds a fourth mode: when a region has marker points but no geoshape or region map image, it shows those points as orange dots on the left-side map so the admin can visually verify the match before accepting. **Scope Fallback** — when Geoshape or Point Match returns no candidates (e.g. island groups whose centroid falls outside GADM polygons), a "Try wider: `<scope>`" link appears inline; clicking it retries the search at the next ancestor level (country → continent → world) without auto-triggering. **Conflict Detection and Accept-With-Transfer** — when a matched GADM division is already assigned to a sibling region, a warning chip ("from Mexico (split Baja California Sur)") marks the suggestion as a conflict. Clicking the map icon or Accept opens a Transfer Preview Dialog showing a three-layer map: the donor region's full geometry in red, the divisions that would move in orange, and the target region outline in dashed blue. The admin clicks "Accept Transfer" to atomically move the divisions and recompute both regions' geometries. **AI Extraction Interview** — during Wikivoyage extraction, when the AI is uncertain (e.g. should a region be split into subregions?), it pauses and asks the admin one structured question with a recommended option; the admin's answer is final for that page (the page is not re-asked) and, when it generalizes, produces a soft rule that informs future extractions. **AI Review Children** — on any region whose Wikivoyage source URL is set, an AI Review button (sparkle icon) audits the region's current child set against its Wikivoyage article. The AI reads the live Wikivoyage wikitext, compares it to existing child regions, and produces a grouped action list: regions to **Add** (missing children from the article), regions to **Remove** (children that no longer appear), and regions to **Rename** (children whose names differ). Each action shows the reasoning and, for Add/Rename actions, a verified Wikivoyage URL. Add and Rename actions are pre-selected by default; Remove actions (destructive) are opt-in. The admin reviews the grouped dialog, checks or unchecks individual items, then clicks Apply to execute all selected actions at once. After all actions complete, the tree refreshes automatically.
- **CV Settings** — a dedicated admin panel to switch the CV color-match pipeline between JavaScript (OpenCV.js WASM) and Python (FastAPI microservice using OpenCV + scikit-image). The setting persists to the database; the Python path is preferred when the service is healthy, with automatic fallback to JavaScript if the service is unreachable.
- **Manage curators** — promote users to curator role, assign scopes (region, source, or global), revoke assignments, and read what one curator has done: their trail opens beside them, each act named in the same words the object's own history uses — “kept out”, “put back”, “took the source’s” — with the recorded payload underneath for the reading that is an audit rather than a story
- **Region assignment** — run the spatial assignment algorithm that maps experiences to regions based on their coordinates
- **Ask the catalogue what it holds** — a *Catalogue Checks* screen putting a set of claims to the live database: a place is not stored twice, an object offered to visitors has somewhere to go, a picture on a page names its photographer, and — since defects in the map keep being found by someone looking at it and by nothing else — where the map thinks a region sits is where it is: a region framed as the whole world while its outline is not, an anchor further than five hundred kilometres from the region it belongs to, a continent drawn as a fraction of its countries, a country with no outline at all, a district standing for the leftover its named towns do not cover while those towns are counted nowhere, and a coarse copy of an outline drawing holes the country itself does not have, or leaving out islands it does. Each says what it found in the words of the thing — "Aldabra Atoll (185): in Seychelles, and in no region" — rather than as a rule id and two numbers, and the ones that need a person are what the page leads with.

  **A number the admin has accepted is quiet.** A catalogue built from four sources over years holds rows nobody is proud of, and a screen that showed every one of them in red every morning is a screen nobody opens twice: 28 objects sit in no region at all today, mostly islands and reefs a boundary set built from land has nowhere to put. So accepting a number records it — with who accepted it and when — and from then on that check reads as debt being carried. What raises attention instead is the number **growing**, which means something is writing those rows now rather than a backlog somebody already knows about; a number that has fallen says so too, so work that fixed something is visible. A check nobody has answered for yet says exactly that, and asks to be answered rather than passing quietly.

  Accepting is one check at a time, deliberately: a single button that accepted everything would make the morning somebody answers for a newly added rule also the morning a real regression got recorded as normal. And the count of visits recorded against a place no longer shown offers no button at all — those records are legitimate, a traveller who stood somewhere stood there, so the number is one to watch rather than a debt to answer for
- **Monitor system health** — sync logs, error tracking, database status

## The Experience System

Experiences are the atomic units of travel engagement — anything a user can discover, learn about, or do in connection with a region. Three experience categories are live: UNESCO World Heritage Sites (~1,250), Museums (~100 top art museums — archaeology, natural-history and military museums are a separate import, not yet built), and Public Art & Monuments (~200). Each carries its own pin colour on the map, and a museum's is its own rather than a heritage site's: they are different reasons to go somewhere. Plans to expand into books, films, food, festivals, wildlife, and many more categories.

Users browse experiences in two views: **Map mode** (select a region, see grouped experiences below) and **Discover mode** (tree-based navigation with map + card list). Multi-location experiences (e.g. serial UNESCO nominations) show in-region locations first, with out-of-region locations collapsed behind a "Show N more" toggle and labeled with their region path (common prefix stripped for brevity). Curators maintain content quality through rejection, editing, and manual creation with AI-assisted lookup.

For the complete experience vision — categories, classification, user interaction, quiz system, context layers, gamification, and implementation phases — see [`EXPERIENCES-OVERVIEW.md`](EXPERIENCES-OVERVIEW.md). For implementation details, see [`experiences.md`](../tech/experiences.md)

## The Region System

Regions are the geographic building blocks. The system supports multiple ways of dividing the world.

### Administrative Divisions (GADM)

The base layer: official country and sub-country boundaries from the GADM database. Pre-simplified at 5 levels of detail for performant map rendering. Forms a strict hierarchy (country → state → district → ...).

### World Views

Custom hierarchical groupings layered on top of GADM divisions. A world view might group countries into continents, cultural regions, or travel-focused zones.

- **Default world view** — browses the administrative divisions themselves, rather than regions grouped on top of them
- **Base layer mirror** — an optional world view imported one region per administrative division, which is what lets experiences attach to plain administrative geography
- **Custom world views** — user-created groupings. A region can contain whole divisions, specific sub-divisions, or custom-drawn boundaries
- **Computed geometry** — region boundaries are automatically computed from their member divisions, with hull algorithms for scattered geography display

### Map Rendering

Vector tiles served by Martin (PostGIS-native tile server). The frontend uses MapLibre GL with react-map-gl for:

- Choropleth coloring of regions (visited/unvisited) with clear visual hierarchy — selected, hovered, visited, and default states each have distinct fill and outline intensities
- A continent is drawn whole. Four of the eight top-level regions of the administrative base layer used to draw a fraction of themselves — North America was Mexico, Central America and the Caribbean, with the United States and Greenland not in it at all; selecting Asia framed from Cyprus to Sulawesi and left out Japan and the whole Russian Far East. Nothing looked broken: a continent that draws part of itself looks like a continent. The cause was the order things were computed in — the countries split across two continents got their outlines days after the continents were built from the ones that already had them, and nothing ever went back. Now a region that gains an outline hands the news upward: every region above it that the map builds out of its parts is redrawn on the next pass, and until it is, the map shows nothing there rather than something wrong. A boundary a curator drew by hand is the exception, and stays exactly as drawn — it was never traced from what is inside it, so nothing inside it can make it wrong. And when a run cannot pass the news up — a lock lost to something else working on the same rows — it says so: the curator's completion line turns amber and carries the count, instead of reading a green "Complete!" over a continent it quietly left wrong. A union that simply will not finish is a different answer: the line counts that continent among the skipped, and the next run comes back to it. If something underneath it got through first, the map shows nothing there rather than something wrong; if nothing did, it keeps the outline it had until some run finally finishes — and either way the Catalogue Checks screen names it, so it is never a continent nobody is looking at
- A country is drawn without holes in it. North-eastern Thailand used to show white patches inside the fill around Nakhon Ratchasima, Surin, Khon Kaen and dozens of other district seats — not water, not somebody else's land, just nothing, in a country whose boundaries tile it without a gap. Uruguay was worse: sixteen of its departments drew only the countryside outside their municipios, so all 125 municipios were gaps — Bella Unión, Carmelo, Colonia Valdense and the rest, each named for its town and holding the farmland around it, from four square kilometres to two and a half thousand. The cause was in the reading rather than the drawing. The boundary file leaves some units unnamed, always the same kind: the part of a place its named pieces do not cover, which in Uruguay is the countryside outside any municipio and in Thailand is whichever sub-districts the file does not name. The loader recognised a place by its name, so it handed each nameless one to the place above and stopped there. That place then stood for the leftover alone while its named pieces hung underneath it, counted by nobody — so the province, the country and the continent each drew around a hole where those pieces are. A place with no name is still a place now: it is loaded as itself and everything above it is drawn including it. It is called after the place it sits in, since that is the only name the source offers — and where it ends up standing beside that place's named parts, it says which one it is: under the Thai district of Hang Dong there is now a tambon called Hang Dong and, beside it, "Hang Dong (rest)", the 71 square kilometres the named ones leave over; under the Uruguayan department of Artigas, "Artigas (rest)" is the countryside its three municipios do not cover. What was already loaded wrong is repaired rather than rebuilt, since the pieces above were never wrong about anything except what they were missing
- The map draws the holes the data has, and no others. North-eastern Thailand kept white blobs after the ones above were closed — 66 of them where the data holds 8, the largest over five hundred square kilometres, which at the zoom a visitor reads a continent at is a lake-sized patch of nothing in the middle of Isan. These were not in any boundary. Drawing a continent at every zoom means keeping coarser copies of its outline, and the pass that made them worked on each piece of coastline separately: two pieces that did not touch could be moved until they overlapped, and the tidy-up that follows every such change resolves an overlap by cutting it out — as a hole. So a continent grew holes as it was made smaller, and grew more of them the further out you zoomed. The outlines are now coarsened as one shape rather than piece by piece, so nothing can be moved across anything else, and every copy carries the lakes and enclaves its country has and nothing invented. Small islands were being quietly thrown away by the same pass, and they are back on every copy that is supposed to carry them: Asia was losing 436 of its 26,151 pieces. (The two coarsest copies still leave out pieces below their own scale, deliberately and as described below — that is a choice about what a reader can see at that zoom, not a copy losing what it was given.) Both halves are now watched on the admin panel, so a future upgrade of the mapping library that started dropping islands again would be named rather than noticed years later by somebody looking at Indonesia
- Drill-down navigation (click a region to see sub-regions)
- Ancestor context layers — when drilling into a region hierarchy, all ancestor levels remain faintly visible as dimmed background layers (root siblings, parent siblings, grandparent siblings, etc.), providing full spatial orientation and clickable navigation back to any level
- Experience density on the overview, individual markers up close. Zoomed out, a region reads as a heat surface: where its experiences concentrate, and how strongly, in one glance — a continent shows its centres of gravity instead of a count in a bubble, and zooming in resolves each area into the finer structure inside it. From the marker threshold every experience is a pin of its own. Every experience in a region is represented either way, however many there are, and hovering one in the list rings it on the map
- Region outline persists during exploration mode as a subtle geographic border, giving spatial context alongside experience markers
- A region that straddles the date line is framed like any other. Pick the Far Eastern Federal District and the map settles over the Sea of Okhotsk with Magadan in the middle of the screen; pick Fiji and it settles between Viti Levu and Vanua Levu. Until recently both answered with the whole world at its furthest zoom, the region itself torn in half and pushed against the left and right edges, and the camera pointed at open water on the other side of the planet — Fiji's frame was centred a thousand kilometres off Namibia. It was one wrong measurement, not two: the countries' outlines reach a fraction of a millimetre past the date line, enough for the boundary check to read them as covering every longitude on Earth. Antarctica, which really does, still gets the whole world, because for a continent wrapped around the pole there is no window that would be a frame. Framing works the same way in either world view now, too: a place chosen from the administrative base layer is framed by the same rule as one chosen from a curated hierarchy, where before the base layer had no such rule at all and every dateline region came out as the whole globe. And choosing a place on that base layer no longer downloads its whole outline just to work out where to point the camera — the Far Eastern Federal District's is 17 MB — because where a place is has been worked out once and kept, the way it always was for a curated region. The same rule now frames every other map in the product: an object whose places sit either side of the date line — on the main map and in Discover — is shown as the stretch of ocean between them rather than the whole planet, and the previews a curator or an admin opens while editing a world view frame a Pacific division the way the main map does, where each of them used to measure the shape its own way and get the globe
- Zoomed all the way out, the map draws a coarser rendering of each region than it does up close — detail no one can see at that scale is not fetched, so the world appears in about a second rather than the twenty to twenty-five it took when every coastline was cut down to size on each request. Every region still appears, however small: a region's largest piece of land is kept whatever the scale, so the smallest country on the map is still on it
- The coarse world map is a map, not a mosaic. Borders hold together now: Scandinavia is a coastline rather than a handful of shards, and the Baltic has no white gashes through it — where before, each region was thinned on its own and the two sides of one border came apart by tens of kilometres. Some pairs are still thinned separately, across a country's edge and between the continents at the very top, and there the two sides differ by around a kilometre — a fraction of a pixel at the scales this affects, rather than the tens of kilometres it used to be. (The administrative base layer is not there yet: its own boundaries are still thinned region by region.) What the coarsening leaves out is an offshore island too small to read at the scale in question — a couple of screen pixels or less. It reappears as you zoom in: Bornholm from the third zoom, Gozo from the fifth. A region that *is* an island or a scattering of them always keeps its largest one, so the Isle of Man, Gotland, Saaremaa and Sardinia are drawn at every scale and nothing disappears from the map entirely
- Between the world view and a country's own scale — the zooms where you pick out a continent and then a country — the map now arrives twice as fast on the busiest layer and up to nineteen times faster on the continent outlines, because it stopped fetching detail finer than the screen can draw
- A wait for map data never traps the viewer. If tiles have not arrived after a few seconds, or fail outright, the covering "Loading map…" panel steps aside, the map becomes pannable and zoomable, and a small note says some areas are still loading — rather than an opaque screen with no way forward
- The map's own furniture — zoom and compass controls, attribution, popups — is styled by a stylesheet the application ships with, not one fetched from a public CDN as the page loads. A visitor on a network that blocks that CDN, or one who arrives during an outage of it, sees the same map as everyone else rather than controls stripped bare of their styling. It also means the version of those styles can no longer drift away from the version of the map library actually in use
- A browser that cannot draw maps at all says so, once, in plain terms. Maps are drawn with WebGL, and some machines have it switched off — hardware acceleration disabled, or a graphics driver the browser distrusts. Where a map would be, such a visitor now reads that it cannot be displayed, why, and that turning on hardware acceleration brings it back, along with a line about what still works on that particular screen: the region tree and experience lists never needed WebGL, and a location can still be set by search, coordinates or description instead of by clicking. Previously this was invisible in the worst way — the main map claimed areas were "still loading" and that panning worked, neither of which was true, and Discover took the whole page down with it

## Design Principles

1. **Reflection over logging** — we help people remember and appreciate where they've been, not passively track their location
2. **Play over data entry** — quizzes, visual maps, and badges make tracking fun rather than tedious
3. **Depth over breadth** — connection levels reward deep engagement with a few places over superficial visits to many
4. **Local expertise** — curators bring regional knowledge; future locals' perspective features amplify authentic voices
5. **Cultural respect** — no ranking cultures or experiences. Cultural relativism guides how we present context
6. **Open data** — built on UNESCO, Wikidata, GADM, and other open sources. Users can export their data
