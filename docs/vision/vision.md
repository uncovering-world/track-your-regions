# Track Your Regions — Project Vision

## The Idea

Track Your Regions is a travel memory and discovery platform. It turns the question "where have you been?" into a rich, interactive experience — not just pins on a map, but a living record of your relationship with places around the world.

The core insight: travel is not binary. You don't just "visit" a place — you might pass through it on a train, explore its back streets for a week, or know everything about it from books without ever setting foot there. The app models this spectrum of connection and gives users tools to explore, track, and deepen their engagement with the world.

## User Roles

### Visitor (Not Authenticated)

A visitor can:

- **Browse the map** — see the world divided into regions (countries, sub-regions, custom groupings) rendered as interactive vector tiles
- **Find what a region holds, not only what the world knows — built, not yet shown** — the Iconic places of a kind are what the whole world writes about, and the world writes about Rome and Berlin far more than about Bali, Salvador or Kyoto's temples. The catalogue now carries a second way in for each kind of place, read from Wikidata's own data: a temple, a church, a tower, a bridge or a square that the travel guides or the Wikipedias single out among the places within 50 km of it — admitted for how it compares with its own surroundings rather than with the world (ADR-0171), without an Iconic badge and for a curator to read first, with the comparison written on it. It is switched off today: at its first numbers it would bring some twenty thousand places for a curator over the world, and how many a dense region should offer is still being decided (#2177), so a visitor sees what the world tier shows and nothing more until it is turned on.
- **See a kind of place across the whole world** — before you choose anywhere, the map is the catalogue: every World Heritage site, or every art museum, or every one of them at once, drawn where they actually are. It used to be blank until you picked a region, so the one question a map is best at answering — *where in the world is this kind of thing?* — could only be answered by opening the region tree and reading counts a region at a time. Now Archaeology is hot around the Mediterranean and the Fertile Crescent and thin across the interior of Africa and most of South America, and you can see that in a glance instead of inferring it. Zoomed out it is a density map; zoomed in the individual places appear, each in its kind's own colour, and pointing at one names it. Clicking one takes you to it — to the smallest region that holds it, with its card open — the same journey the search makes. The places no region has claimed are drawn here too: the Great Barrier Reef and the Wadden Sea are on a map at last, where before they were on none. There is no region to open for those, so clicking one flies the map to it instead of doing nothing — you are shown where it is even while nothing yet files it anywhere. And a sprawling site can be folded, for the whole map at once rather than one row at a time: the rock art of the Iberian Mediterranean is 734 shelters, and unfolded they make eastern Spain look like the densest heritage in Europe, which is true of places and false of sites. The kind you are looking at is part of the link, so a world map of Archaeology is something you can send somebody
- **Explore experiences** — browse UNESCO World Heritage Sites, top museums, public art, monuments & memorials, places of worship organized by region through the Discover page. A museum's list of works is what the source still places there: a work that moved to another museum, or left the record, leaves the wall on the next believed run rather than staying listed for ever, and comes back the moment the source places it here again. One exception keeps a work on screen somewhere: when it has moved to a museum whose new listing a curator has not yet published, it stays on the old wall until that listing is published, rather than vanishing from both. **The filter above a museum's works finds what the screen shows.** A source can spell a title with two spaces inside — Wikidata does for *St. John on Patmos* in the Gemäldegalerie — and the page collapses them, so a visitor who typed the title they were reading got nothing back and no hint why. A name is now stored as a person would type it, wherever it came from, and the filter above the works and the search over a region's places match by what is shown: a dash typed as a hyphen finds the row, and so does a name pasted off a wrapped line. A World Heritage name reads as the site writes it, never with the list's HTML tags around a foreign term: *Via Appia. Regina Viarum*, *Fujian Tulou*, and *SG̱ang Gwaay* with its Haida letter, where the list's own markup used to show on screen
- **Find the monuments apart from the statues** — public art and monuments are two lists: a sculpture, a fountain or a mural is public art, in teal; a memorial, a triumphal arch, a column, an obelisk is a monument, in slate. A place that is both, the Motherland Calls or Nelson's Column, is on both lists as one place. A lake, a road or a house that Wikidata calls a monument — Sanabria Lake, the Way of Saint James, the Anne Frank House — is on neither, and a memorial built as a guardhouse or laid out as a cemetery — the Neue Wache, Arlington — is a memorial.
- **Walk into the churches, mosques and temples too** — a fourth list beside the World Heritage sites, the museums and the public art: the places of worship the world knows by name, with a rose pin of their own and their own count in every region. The Hagia Sophia, Angkor Wat, Notre-Dame de Paris, the Sagrada Família, Prague's St Vitus, Kyoto's Kiyomizu-dera, the Golden Temple at Amritsar — about 1,080 of them at the line this source draws, from Italy, France and Turkey through India, Japan and China rather than one continent's. They arrive by a curator's hand rather than straight onto the map: this is a source anyone in the world can edit, so its first rows wait for a person to read them before a visitor sees any of them. Ten words say what each one is, and you can filter by any of them: cathedral, church, chapel, monastery, mosque, temple, shrine, synagogue, and for a holy place that is no building, sacred mountain or holy site. A place none of them fits says nothing rather than being forced into the nearest word — Po-i-Kalyan in Bukhara and the Alamo Mission are simply places of worship — and where a guidebook and a class list disagree the guidebook wins: Wat Pho is a temple, not a monastery. The list is holy places, not only buildings: the Western Wall and Ahu Tongariki are holy sites, and Kailash, Fuji, Mount Kōya and Mount Arafat are sacred mountains — a mountain held sacred that no faith or pilgrimage is named for, such as Kanchenjunga or Rainier, is left to the mountains, and a holy object is the place it is kept in: the Black Stone and the Kaaba are Al-Masjid Al-Haram's, the Pergamon Altar its museum's. A place under the line that a year of readers puts at 25,000 comes in too, for the curator to read first — Mont-Saint-Michel Abbey, the Sanctuary of Fátima, the Virupaksha Temple at Hampi. A few visitable places are still missing until somebody adds them by hand: Meteora, Shah-i-Zinda and the Batu Caves carry no holy class, and a handful of ruins you can buy a ticket for — Champmol, the Abbey of St Victor, the rebuilt Bibi-Heybat Mosque — are on record as destroyed
- **A place that has something to look at inside says so** — a small "3 treasures inside" beside a church's name, in the list by the map, on its Discover card and on either hover card, so you can tell the cathedral you would walk past from the one holding the thing you came to the city for. It is never shown for an art museum, where every row has works and the card already says how many. And what is inside a church is on its card the way a museum's paintings are, each with the photographer named: Michelangelo's *Pietà* at St Peter's, the *Last Supper* at Santa Maria delle Grazie, the *Ghent Altarpiece* at St Bavo's, the *Ecstasy of Saint Teresa* at Santa Maria della Vittoria, the Shroud of Turin at the cathedral, the Iron Crown at Monza, Strasbourg's astronomical clock. A tower is not one of them: nobody goes into Pisa Cathedral to see the Leaning Tower, so the tower is neither a place of worship nor the cathedral's treasure, and it waits for the kind that is its own — what you climb, or stand under in a square. A work one list turns down because it stands in a church or a museum is not lost to the others: it goes on the card of the church or museum that holds it — the Horses of Saint Mark on St Mark's Basilica, the Bamberg Horseman in Bamberg Cathedral — and the run that put it there says which list handed it over. And a monument that stands outdoors on the grounds of a museum the catalogue lists in no kind stays a monument rather than vanishing with it: the Mother Ukraine statue on its hill in Kyiv is public art, whatever the war museum below it is. A clock that puts on a show for the street is public art too, and not a tower, with a Clock chip of its own: crowds gather under Prague's Astronomical Clock on the hour, while the Old Town Hall it hangs on is in no list, and Munich's Glockenspiel and Olomouc's clock are offered though few Wikipedias write about them, since the world reads them. A work you go to a museum to see stays the museum's: the *Creation of Adam* is the Vatican Museums', so the Sistine Chapel is in the list on its own fame, with nothing of ours hanging in it. Cologne Cathedral is a World Heritage site and a cathedral, and it is one place with one pin, listed under both. A place that is one part of a World Heritage site of many — the Karnak Temple Complex, one of Ancient Thebes' places — becomes that site's part once the catalogue knows the two by one Wikidata item, and stays a place of its own: it keeps its own card and list row and names the site it is part of, though on the world map its spot still carries the site's pin beside its own. A dig says the same about what came out of it, which is a different thing to know before you go: "4 finds on view" beside the Acropolis of Athens, "2 finds on view" beside Mycenae — in the list, on the Discover card and on either hover card — so a region's list of sites tells Amarna, whose Nefertiti is in Berlin, from a site whose finds are nowhere a traveller can see them; the card, once opened, names the museums
- **See what exists** — understand the breadth of experiences available in any region, with counts, kinds of place, images, and descriptions. Every place says what it is in one line under its name — Ajanta Caves reads "2nd century BCE to 1st century CE Buddhist cave monuments located in Maharashtra, India", the Hermitage "museum in Saint Petersburg, Russia" — in the list by the map, on its Discover card and on either hover card, and opening it shows the paragraph that describes it, such as UNESCO's for a World Heritage site, with a line under it saying whose text it is: "Text: UNESCO World Heritage Centre · CC BY-SA 4.0", the licence UNESCO publishes it under, or "adapted from" it where a curator has edited the paragraph. A click on the map opens the place the hover card named, even where two places' marks overlap — the Serapeum of Alexandria and Pompey's Pillar stand 81 m apart. A region is shown whole rather than as a first page of it: the map draws every experience assigned there, and the list reaches all of them — it answers about what the map is showing, with one line offering the rest of the region back. Neither is a first page, so a kind is never represented only by its alphabetically-early members. Whole no longer means slow: a region holding hundreds of places used to lock the tab for a couple of seconds while it drew every row at once, and now draws the rows you are looking at as you scroll — the same list, the same scrollbar covering all of it, without the freeze. Opening a place's card no longer makes the list shuffle underneath you either. A card is not shown until everything in it is ready, so it opens once, at the size it keeps, and the rest of the list stays where you left it — where before it grew in steps and then jumped again when the picture arrived. While a card is being fetched the row you clicked says so quietly, without moving, and if your pointer rested on that row for a moment beforehand its picture is already waiting, which is the slowest part of the wait by far. An open card of a sprawling site now shows the first twenty of its places with a line offering the rest — the Historic Centre of Saint Petersburg has 112, and listing all of them at once was half a second of work before the card could appear. Running your pointer down that list is answered at once, on the row and on the map alike: it used to lag by three to five rows and light the map a second or two later, which made a card of many places feel stuck rather than slow. Closing a card leaves the map where you put it, too — it used to fly back out to the whole region, throwing away the view you had zoomed into and, with it, changing which rows the list was showing you
- **Switch world views** — see the world organized in different ways: any custom regional grouping an admin has published. Administrative geography is reachable this way only if an admin publishes a world view built from it (see the base layer import below); the built-in administrative hierarchy itself stays admin-only. The address bar follows the choice and the choice follows the address bar: a link carrying a world view opens on it, and pasting or editing one in a tab that is already open switches to it
- **Send someone a place** — a place has an address. The Historic Centre of Saint Petersburg is a link, and so is Europe, and so is the list of a country's World Heritage Sites in Discover: whoever opens it lands on that place, with the card already open on top of its region, rather than at the top of a continent with instructions to find it again. The map shows that region at once rather than flying in from the whole world, which showed nothing but the map loading at every zoom on the way; moving from one region to the next still flies, because the movement says where the next one lies. Refreshing the tab keeps you where you were, and Back walks the places you actually chose — the region you came from, the card you closed — rather than every twitch of the map. The link names the place in plain words (`/wv/5/r/6737-europe/e/1234-stonehenge`), and the browser tab and any preview of the link say what it is. What holds the link together is the object, not the wording: a site renamed by a correction or a curator keeps every link ever shared of it, and the words in the address quietly catch up. The same holds when two entries turn out to be one place: once the Pantheon the catalogue held as a place of worship and as an archaeological site is one place, a link shared to either opens that one place's card. One place of a serial site — the Monastery of Haghpat of the Monasteries of Haghpat and Sanahin, the Temple of Karnak of Ancient Thebes — is a place with a link of its own too, and it opens the site's card at that place, with its photograph, its own Wikipedia article where it has one — Neuschwanstein's, not the article about King Ludwig's palaces as a whole — the site it is part of and the step to the site's next place; its row in the site's list links the same article. A part that is also a place in a kind of its own opens its own card: once the Temple of Karnak is known to be the Karnak Temple Complex the catalogue holds as a place of worship, a link to the temple opens the complex, which still names Ancient Thebes as the site it is part of. A link to something you are not allowed to see — a world view an admin has not published, a place that no longer exists — does not say so; it simply opens the nearest thing you can see, because a link should never become a way to find out what is being kept from you. Nothing about you goes into a link either: where you have been is yours, and addresses travel through other people's logs and chat windows
- **Take a place's name and pages with you** — the name on an open card can be selected and copied like any text without the card snapping shut under the pointer, and the place's pages read as words rather than as icons to guess at: "Wikipedia", "Official website", and on a World Heritage site "UNESCO page", each a link the browser can copy. The arrow at the end of a row is the button that opens and closes the card, which a keyboard can now reach
- **Share a place from its card** — an open card on the map has a *Share* button beside *Mark Visited*. On a phone it opens the phone's own share sheet with the card's link and the place's name; on a computer without one it copies the link and says "Link copied". The link opens the same region with the card already open, and carries nothing about the person who sent it
- **Get there with your own maps app** — on the map and in Discover, a card for a place of one point offers it to OpenStreetMap and Google Maps, and first to the phone's own maps app: Apple Maps on an iPhone, the app you chose on Android. A site of many offers the same on each place's row behind one map icon, and a part's own card offers it for that part. It works for every place, a village chapel or an excavation with no street address included, and nothing about you goes to a map provider until you press one. If you always navigate with one of them, say so on the **Account** page (*Open places in*), reached from your own menu or, signed in or not, from *Choose a maps app for every place* beside the maps links: each card then offers that one, with the others behind a small *Other maps* menu. Signed in, the choice goes with your account to every device; signed out, this browser keeps it
- **Read about a work** — a notable work on a card, Madonna with Canon Joris van der Paele in Groeningemuseum say, carries a "Wikipedia" link at the end of its line, so reading about the painting or sending it to a friend no longer means retyping its title into a search engine. The link opens the English article; a work with no English article, such as the stele of Lemnos, shows no link rather than a page saying there is none. Its name stays plain text you can select
- **Find something by name** — a visitor who knows what they are looking for can now type it. The box above the region list answers with both halves of the question, each under its own heading: the regions whose names match, and the places themselves. One click opens the place's card in the region that holds it, and in the *smallest* region that holds it — the Rijksmuseum belongs to Noord-Holland, to the Netherlands and to Europe alike, and it opens in Noord-Holland, so the map frames the museum rather than the continent. The card opens even when its kind was the one folded shut, which a link to a card had never quite managed before. The search looks through the whole catalogue rather than through the world view on screen, and it never moves you out of the world view you chose: what this one cannot open is still listed, quietly, saying which of the two things happened — another world view holds it, or it is not on a map yet. Things in that second state are not obscure ones — the Great Barrier Reef and the Wadden Sea are there, sites whose real extent no region has claimed yet — and saying so is a better answer than "no results" for something the catalogue plainly has.
- **What just arrived** — a **New** mark on things you could first see recently, not on things that happen to be recent rows in a database. It stays for a while — how long depends on the source, since some publish monthly and some rarely — and then it fades on its own, per thing. It is not tied to a batch: a source that updates weekly shows several weeks' worth of new things at a time rather than only its latest run's, and a thing held back for review carries its mark from the day you could see it rather than from the day an importer found it
- **One answer to where something is** — a place you can actually go to. Some sites are one thing in a list and another on the map: the world's official register publishes a point for the *property*, which for a rainforest spread across a state can be 191 km from any part of it you could visit. What you are shown now is never that locator: it is a real place of the site. A site made of many parts is drawn as its parts, on the map and in Discover alike — the rainforest is its forty forests, the rock art is the shelters you can walk to — so what the map says is here is what is here. Where one point is unavoidable, it is the real part nearest the middle of the site's parts — the same part however the source orders its list, so the Danube Limes no longer jumps from Carnuntum to Linz — and a curator is not asked about it when the source moves it, because a site of many parts has no single place of its own; the review card says what the site is made of instead of printing that point
- **The list beside the map follows it** — zoomed into a city, the list answers "what is here" rather than reading the whole region alphabetically: Europe offers 661 experiences, and a reader looking at Prague was given them from *Historic Centre of Prague* to *Wikipedia Monument* by initial. The rows are the ones the map is showing, each kind says how many of its own are here — "12 of 467", the second number being the whole region's — and one line offers the rest of the region back, counting what the view is hiding. A card you have opened is the exception: it stays in the list wherever you then move the map, because a pan should not close what you are reading, and the list re-aims at it if the map's own flight moved it off screen. Zoomed out far enough that the whole region is on screen, the two questions have the same answer: every row is listed, the kind counts show one number instead of two, and the line offering the rest of the region does not appear, because nothing is missing from the list. Discover's own list is unchanged: it is browsed by region rather than beside a map you are steering.
- **Fold a sprawling site back into one pin** — some sites are hundreds of places: the rock art of the Iberian Mediterranean is 734 shelters, the Roman frontier 420 forts and walls. Drawn in full they are the honest answer to "what is here", and they can also be more than you wanted while you look at everything else. Any one of them can be folded back to a single pin — from the map on either view, and from the row itself when you are browsing regions — and only that one: the rest of the region stays drawn as the places it is. A folded pin keeps a small count so it never reads as a place on its own, and unfolds with a click. The fold lasts as long as you are looking at that region — it is not a setting to remember or to find again
- **A site in trouble says so** — 58 of the world's heritage sites are inscribed on the List of World Heritage in Danger, and the card now says which, and since when: the Ancient City of Aleppo reads **In Danger since 2013**, the Ancient City of Tauric Chersonese since 2026. The date is the part that means something — a thirteen-year emergency is not the same news as this year's — and it is the source's own, not a guess. The mark had been in the product since the beginning and had appeared for nobody: the catalogue was reading the wrong half of what the official list sends, so every one of those 58 sites sat here looking like any other. A site the world takes back *off* that list loses the mark with it, and a site taken off the World Heritage List altogether, as Liverpool was in 2021, loses both the mark and the year it was inscribed: they are facts about an inscription that no longer stands, so the card no longer shows them
- **Places that changed** — some sites leave the official lists without going anywhere. Those stay exactly where they were, on the map and in the lists, with a small **Former** mark and the reason: no longer listed, still there to visit. The mark belongs to the list that dropped the place: a site UNESCO delisted reads Former among World Heritage Sites, and current in any other kind of place that still lists it. Places that genuinely no longer exist are a different matter — offering somewhere demolished as somewhere to go would be a lie, so they leave the lists, the map and the counts. A region that holds any says so at the end of its list — on the map, and in Discover, where the count is of the kind being browsed — and one click brings them back for anyone curious what used to be there; a curator who finds one wrongly recorded can then say it does still exist, from either list
- **Only what a person has passed** — some sources are trusted outright; others, ones more prone to importer mistakes, are held back until a curator has looked. Once a source is gated, anything it wrote that nobody has checked yet is simply absent — no card, no marker, no line in a count — the same as if it did not exist. This applies at whatever size the gate catches a mistake: a whole new venue, one of its locations, or one work inside an otherwise-visible museum can each be held back on its own, without hiding the rest of what that source already offers. What being held back never does is take something away: when a gated source moves a place, the pin you have been seeing stays where it is until the new one has been checked, rather than the map going blank while a corrected coordinate waits for someone to look at it. So something that has ever been shown to you never falls off the map entirely while its source still offers it — an old pin left standing a little too long is the cost, and it is the one worth paying. Newly arrived points are a different matter and are simply not there yet, so a place that has just gained locations shows fewer of them than its source lists until a curator has looked. A region reaches a site through its points, so a point nobody has passed does not put its site into a region either: were a seventh temple of the Megalithic Temples of Malta to arrive in Ċentrali — the one Maltese region the other six are not in — that region would not list the site, count it, or draw a pin for it until somebody had read the temple. The three answers arrive together, on the day it is passed; a region never offers a site with nothing of it to show you
- **"New" means new to you** — the mark on a card says a visitor could first see this recently, not that an importer noticed it recently. Those are the same day for a trusted source and can be a week apart for a held one, where an object waits for a curator before anyone can see it at all. Anchoring the mark to the moment it became visible is what stops a held object arriving on your screen already unmarked, having spent its "new" while nobody could see it. Two consequences worth knowing. Marks now fade on their own schedule per object rather than all at once when a source next runs, so a weekly source shows several weeks' worth of new things at a time rather than only its latest batch. And everything that was already on the site before any of this wears no mark at all: nothing recorded when those things first became visible, so the honest answer is silence rather than a guessed date — the mark starts meaning something from the first thing published after the change. A mark is also spent only by a thing that actually reached your screen, not by a list that happened to contain it: opening a region of several hundred places used to count all of them as seen at once, so anything new below the fold quietly spent its "new" while you were still reading the top of the list

### Authenticated User

Everything a visitor can do, plus:

- **Mark regions as visited** — click a region on the map to toggle it as visited, building a personalized travel map. A visit is yours: a curator reorganising a world view cannot delete a region you have marked, so tidying a hierarchy never erases your travels
- **Track experiences** — mark individual UNESCO sites, museums, landmarks and places of worship as visited with a simple checkbox. What you mark is yours to keep: no data refresh deletes it, whether the source renames what it calls a place or stops listing it altogether. A place the source stops offering leaves the lists, while your record of having been there is kept intact. One case is honest to name: when the source **replaces** a point rather than editing it — moves it more than ten metres, or files it under a new reference — that arrives as a new place, so a site you had finished shows as in progress again until you tick the new one. A coordinate merely written more precisely does not do this: within ten metres it is the same point, the same row, and your tick stays on it. Nothing is lost; the tick simply stays on the spot you actually visited. A tick on one place of a serial site — the Monastery of Haghpat — is a visit to that place, and to the site: unticking it keeps the site visited while you have been to another of its places, as you have been to Sanahin. A bronze cast for several cities is one place with a pin at every cast, each in its own country: Louise Bourgeois's *Maman* stands in Bilbao, Ottawa, London and Tokyo, and the map shows all four under one name rather than sending you to whichever one the data happened to list first. Rodin's *The Burghers of Calais* is the same work in Calais, in London's Victoria Tower Gardens and at Stanford, and *The Thinker* stands in the catalogue where its casts do — public art as long as one cast stands where a passer-by can see it, with a pin only at the casts that stand outdoors, while the museum that holds another cast keeps the work in its collection, and left to the museums alone only when every cast is inside one; a study of one figure is a work of its own, not a cast, and so is a work someone else based on it: a parody of *The Thinker* in Greenwich puts no pin of *The Thinker* there. Standing under any one cast is having seen the work: tick *Maman* in Bilbao and it reads visited everywhere, without asking you to reach the other three.
- **See progress** — visual feedback on how much of a region you've explored (experience completion counts). A place that no longer exists drops out of what a region counts as there to see, but never out of what you have seen: your visit to it stays, because you did go
- **Manage world views** — create and edit custom regional hierarchies with drag-and-drop region building, custom subdivision drawing, and AI-assisted boundary creation. In the Create Subregions map, the division under the pointer lights up and names itself and its group, so a click lands where the eye is — including a part a cut has just made, which is the case that used to stay dark
- **Personal travel map** — a world map colored by your visited regions, visible at a glance
- **A New mark that waits for you** — the **New** window is the same for everyone, but signed in it is a floor rather than a deadline: arrive at the tail of it and the mark stays a week longer, counted from the first time it was actually shown to *you*. A batch is not something you can miss by visiting on the wrong day, and nothing a source does later takes the mark away — it runs out on its own

- **Choose your maps app** — the **Account** page's *Maps* section, open to you signed in or not and reached from beside any card's maps links, sets the maps app places open in: *Ask each time*, the device's own, OpenStreetMap or Google Maps
- **Change your password** — the **Account** page, reached from your own menu, shows who you are and lets you change the password you signed up with. It asks for the current one, refuses anything that has turned up in a public breach, and says the number of times it has, because that is what makes the refusal convincing rather than officious. Changing it signs out every *other* device and leaves the one you are on open — the page says so before you press, not after. An account that signs in with Google (or Apple) has no password here, so it is told where its password lives instead of being handed a form that cannot work

The authenticated experience is about reflection and tracking — turning scattered travel memories into a coherent, browsable record.

#### How to Sign Up

Two paths to an account:

- **Email/password** — register with a display name (one that is more than spaces, stored without the spaces around it), email, and password. The app sends a verification email with a one-time link. Clicking the link verifies the email and logs you in. Until verified, login is blocked. The verification link expires after 24 hours; users can request a fresh one via "Resend verification email"
- **OAuth** — sign in with Google (or Apple). No email verification needed — the provider has already verified the address. Instant access

Every password field, at sign-in, at registration and on the Account page, has an eye button at its end that shows what you typed and hides it again; each field is masked when it opens and again once the form is sent, and showing one does not show the others.

The registration form shows a "Check your email" confirmation state after submission, with the option to resend the verification email without leaving the dialog.

### Curator

Everything an authenticated user can do, plus content quality controls scoped to their assigned regions or sources: a curator of one source curates every place that source holds, even a place another source brought first (the Ajanta Caves, brought by UNESCO, are a Places of worship curator's to edit because they are a place of worship too), and answers that source's own questions about a shared place.

- **Reject experiences** — hide incorrectly assigned or low-quality experiences from a region, with an optional reason. Rejections are region-scoped (rejecting from "Europe" doesn't affect "France")
- **Unreject experiences** — reverse a previous rejection if it was a mistake or circumstances changed
- **Edit experiences** — fix names, the line under the name, descriptions, types, image URLs, and link URLs for any experience in their scope, and **take one away**: emptying the picture, the line under the name, the description, the type or a link and saving removes it, and the removal is a curation decision the next sync run respects rather than a gap it fills back in. An experience that sits in several regions counts as in scope when *any* of those regions is one the curator covers — the edit is on the experience, not on its place in one region. The doors to editing, correcting and adding are shown only where the curator's scope reaches, so none of them ends in a refusal: a curator of Tuscany who opens Italy can edit the Uffizi and not the Colosseum, and is offered no way to add a place to Italy itself. For a region-scoped curator the history entry is recorded under a region they cover, so it stays visible in their own view of that history; for a global or source curator, under no region at all, since their authority came from none in particular. The link URL points to a reference page (typically Wikipedia) and is shown as a clickable button in the experience details (next to the visited/curate actions). An image or link URL that is not a web address is refused as it is saved, rather than accepted and then quietly drawing nothing: a link has to be an ordinary `http(s)://…` address, and a picture that or a path to a file this site hosts. A value the system refuses is named under the box it came from, in the system's own wording for now, and the dialog stays open with it; Save wakes only once something has changed, and sends only what did. **The picture being edited is on screen.** Under the Image URL box the dialog draws the picture the address names, with the photographer's name and licence under it, so checking whether the stored photograph is the right building — or what a candidate found on Commons looks like — is no longer a copy, a new tab and a way back. An address typed and not yet saved is previewed without a credit, since none exists until the save resolves one; an emptied box draws nothing; and an address the product may not draw from, or a picture that fails to load, shows no frame and no credit rather than a broken one On a World Heritage site, a curator who rewrites UNESCO's paragraph says whether the new text is an edit of it, which readers then see credited as adapted from UNESCO, or their own, which carries no credit.
- **Set a type the kind has, and read one in the kind's words** — an object's type is a distinction inside its kind that a traveller still browses together: a World Heritage site is cultural, natural or mixed, a piece of public art a sculpture, a fountain, street art or a clock, a monument a memorial, an arch, a column, an obelisk or a monument, a place of worship a cathedral, church, chapel, monastery, mosque, temple, shrine, synagogue, sacred mountain or holy site. The edit and create dialogs offer exactly that list for the object's kind, and a museum offers none — an art museum and an archaeology museum are two kinds, not two types, and the "art" every museum used to carry as its type is gone. On a review card the fact is called *type* (the chip beside the name is the kind), it is one row rather than the same change asked twice, and its explanation is written for the vocabulary the value is from: a monument's card no longer opens with UNESCO's cultural, natural or mixed. An object is drawn in one colour everywhere — its pin, its row, its card — and that colour is its kind's, refined by the type only for World Heritage, so a monument is no longer purple in the list and teal on the map beside it. A place offered in several kinds, opened from the map or a link, shows its card above the groups with one chip per kind, each naming the kind beside the type — the Serapeum of Alexandria reads *Places of worship · Temple* and *Archaeology · Site* — since no group header stands over that card to say which kind a bare *Temple* belongs to; a card opened inside a kind's group keeps the type alone.
- **Fix a place that is in the wrong place** — a curator can correct the name of one location inside an object, or move its point to where the thing actually is. Until now the only answers a held coordinate offered were to take the source's point or to keep it, which between them cannot fix a pin on the wrong building; and any correction made anywhere else was quietly written over the next time the source was asked. A corrected value now stays corrected: the source stops being able to overwrite that one field, while everything else about the point still follows it. Where the object is a single place, its own published coordinate moves with the point: there the two are one fact, and a coordinate left behind would be the object listed under one point and its pin standing at another. Where an object is made of many places — a serial site with forty components — only the component moves, because which of the forty the object as a whole should be pinned to is not something a single correction can answer. A moved point is reassigned to whatever region it now falls in, so correcting a coordinate across a border moves the place into the right country's list too. A correction can also be taken back, and it is taken back whole: where the source keeps proposing its own coordinates and a curator finally says the source was right, the pin moves back to the source's straight away and the object's own position follows at the next sync — handing back half of it would leave the object listed at one coordinate and drawn at another, which is the disagreement this exists to remove. Taking it back is only offered where the object is one place, though: on a serial site with forty components, a corrected component stays corrected, because the question "was the source right about this coordinate?" is asked about the object and there is no card that asks it about one of forty. **The correction is offered wherever a curator whose scope reaches the place is looking at it** — taking one back is not: that stays on the review page's conflict card, where the source's own proposal is what a curator says yes to. Every point the review page shows opens in one dialog — an unread point under a gated object, a part the source wants to move, a withdrawn point — with the pin already in hand: the map opens on the place, the source's own position stays as a faded pin beside it, and saving is offered once something has changed. A place's row on an open card in Map mode and in Discover shows the same door when the row is pointed at; and the object's own screen carries where it is as a field beside its name — for a museum or a monument the one place it has, which had no row anywhere before; for a serial site its parts, folded behind a count. A row that has been corrected says so — *pin corrected*, *name corrected* — and a correction says what it did in one line: how far the pin moved, whether the object's own position moved with it, that the source will no longer overwrite the field, and, for a point readers do not see, what would show it — a withdrawn point is shown by answering "false alarm", an unread one by publishing it, a turned-down one by asking about it again and then publishing it, one the source has since dropped by nothing at all until the source lists it again, and one under an object with its own open question by answering that question first. Nothing here rewrites the source's own coordinate on a serial site: a component is corrected as a component, and which of forty the object as a whole stands at is not a question one correction answers. **A renamed component stays the one the card is about.** A park that crosses a border is listed once per country under one number — Maloti-Drakensberg Park's *Sehlabathebe National Park* and *uKhahlamba Drakensberg Park* — and the card telling the two apart by name meant that renaming one from the card handed its open question to the other: the card reopened on the neighbour, and publishing would have written the source's name onto it. The corrected component now keeps its question, and publishing lands on the place the curator looked at. Where nothing can tell the two apart — both carrying a curator's name — the card offers no door for that part, and publishing says so and leaves the question standing rather than guessing.
- **Correct who made a work, and say who led it** — a work in a museum can be re-attributed, retitled or re-dated by a curator, and what they write is theirs from then on: the next import leaves those fields alone. That includes the *order* of the makers, which no import can supply: writing them in the order a curator chooses is what lets the rest of the site say "Andy Warhol and 5 others" instead of counting them, and the admin panel's Catalogue Checks says how many works are still waiting for somebody to decide. This is the answer the review queue had no button for. Its two are "take the source's value" and "keep what is here", and neither of them says *this instead* — so a curator looking at the Borghese Gladiator, which Wikidata attributes to Nicolas Cordier, who restored an arm of it in the 17th century, rather than to Agasias of Ephesus, who carved it, could only choose which of two wrong answers to keep. Saying nobody is known is an answer too: *Salvator Mundi* credited to "Leonardeschi" tells a traveller less than no name at all. Because a work hangs in more than one museum, a correction made from one is what every museum holding it carries — the same rule as passing a work, which is done once for the whole catalogue, and the screen says so before anything is saved: *The Great Wave off Kanagawa* is one row and eleven museums, each holding an impression of the same print. **The correction is offered wherever a curator whose scope reaches its place is looking at a work** — the works waiting to be published on the review page, the part a run wants to change, and a work's row on an open card in Map mode and in Discover, each showing the door when the row is pointed at or reached by keyboard. It opens already editing: the makers are names to drag or step through with arrows rather than a line of text to retype, so putting three of them in order costs no risk of a typo in a name nobody meant to change; a curator who thinks the stored order is already right can vouch for it without changing anything, which is what most of the works waiting are actually waiting for. The year is written the way a person writes one — 100 BC rather than a minus sign — which matters more than it sounds: the oldest thing in the catalogue is a mammoth-ivory lion from Museum Ulm carved around 38000 BC. The picture is correctable here too, and never on its own: the photographer's name is looked up for whatever file is named and stored with it, so no work ever appears under one photograph credited to whoever took another. Where that lookup names nobody — Commons holds files whose author it does not record, and the wait for an answer is bounded at five seconds — the picture is kept and the screen says no photographer was found, rather than leaving a curator to assume one exists. A row that has been corrected says so — *title corrected*, *makers confirmed* — and a correction says what it did in one line, including who was credited for a new picture, or that nobody could be
- **Add existing experiences** — search the global database and manually assign an experience to their region when automatic spatial assignment missed it. Available via the Search & Add tab in the add dialog
- **Create new experiences** — add landmarks, venues, or points of interest that don't exist in any synced source. Each kind's group header in the experience list has a "+" button that opens the Create New dialog pre-set to that kind (e.g. clicking "+" on "Art Museums" pre-selects the Art Museums kind). The curator must choose which kind to file the place under — there is no generic "Curator Picks" kind — and the place is filed under that kind's own source, the one that fills it. Once the curator has typed a name (3+ characters), *Look up* beside it — or Enter in the field — finds the place and fills in coordinates, a Wikidata image, a short description, and a Wikipedia link. The location is where the matched Wikidata item itself says the place is, so "Charles Bridge" added under *Europe* lands on the bridge in Prague; OpenStreetMap's place search is the fallback for an item that states no location, and its place wins over an item matched only by the bare name somewhere else: Brno's Old Town Hall is neither moved to Hanover's nor given Hanover's photograph, description or Wikipedia link — the dialog says no item matched the place and names Hanover's as one that only shares the name, leaving those fields for the curator. It is searched with the current region's name first, for disambiguation ("Old Town Hall" in the Czechia region finds the one in Brno, not the one in Hertfordshire), and by the name alone when that finds nothing — a region such as a continent or "Balkans" is no part of an address. When the lookup finds no location, the dialog says so beside the match, until the curator sets one. It never runs on its own while the curator types: the place search behind it is OpenStreetMap's Nominatim, whose terms forbid search-as-you-type, and a lookup started by a pause in typing used to search half a name. After it runs, the curator can freely edit the display name without losing the filled-in metadata; a suggestion info box shows the matched Wikidata entity, and *Look up again* replaces what the last lookup filled in. All fields remain fully editable, and a "Suggest" button provides manual image lookup as fallback. Location can also be set manually via a multi-mode picker: click on a map, search by place name (Enter or *Find*, with OpenStreetMap credited under the results), paste or type coordinates in any format (decimal, DMS, Google Maps URL — the pin follows as they are typed, and the field is tidied into one format only once the curator leaves it), or describe the place in natural language for AI geocoding. A thumbnail preview shows below the image URL field. After creation, the dialog closes automatically and the new experience appears immediately in the list and on the map. Closed before that, it keeps the half-filled place for the next opening — the kind and type the curator chose included, and which fields the lookup filled, so replacing the name still moves what the lookup placed rather than leaving the first place's pin and picture under a new name; only opening it from a different kind's "+" switches the kind
- **Remove from region** — permanently remove a rejected experience from the region entirely (deletes the assignment). Unlike rejection (which hides), removal cleans the experience out of the region's list. The rejection record is kept as a guard — if a future spatial recompute re-adds the experience, it will automatically be hidden again. Available as an icon button in the rejected list (Map mode) and as a button in the curation dialog (Discover mode)
- **See rejected items** — rejected experiences appear dimmed with strikethrough in their list, invisible to regular users
- **Open an object nobody has checked yet** — a curator or admin whose scope reaches an experience can still open its page, its locations and its treasures directly by address, even while its source is gated and everyone else is shown nothing there. Every list, count, search and the map stay exactly as a visitor sees them either way — this only changes what happens at the object's own address, not what the catalogue is measured to hold
- **Work the queue as a list, not as a stack** — the review page is the questions on the left and the one under decision on the right. It was a single column of cards, and the cards themselves are why that stopped working: each now carries what its decision rests on — both versions of a disputed text in full, the object with its picture and its place, the works a rule counted — so nineteen of them stacked meant reaching the twentieth question by scrolling past nineteen answers' worth of evidence. A line in the list carries what a curator decides to *skip* by, and the rest waits on the right. Answering moves to the question that took its place rather than back to the top. Every kind is in one list, newest first under day headings or grouped by question, with one *Show more* at its foot; a heading counts what sits under it only once the whole list is loaded, and until then says nothing rather than calling a page a day. A row states which source and which question before it is opened — *UNESCO World Heritage Sites · **holds a change**: criteria*, *Monuments & memorials · **new arrival***, *· **refused**: 17 Wikipedia editions: below the world tier's line* — and `j` and `k` step down and up it without the mouse. **Finding, ordering and putting down.** A search box finds an object by name across every kind at once; chips narrow the list by source, by question, by region — including *Unplaced*, for the objects placement has left in no region — and by the run that asked, each option saying how many questions picking it would leave. The region chip offers the continents and goes deeper on request: type two letters and it finds any region of any public world view by name, each with its path, so a curator working through Berlin reads Berlin's questions and nobody else's, and a link to that list says *Berlin* on the chip. A run is a batch, and the run chip says so in one row: *UNESCO World Heritage Sites · 5 Sep 21:01*, run 98, **1 255 open** — one run of 5 September proposing the same field, the inscription criteria, on 1 255 sites at once. *Set aside this run* puts that batch out of the way until its questions are answered, which on the development catalogue leaves 366 questions in front of the curator instead of 1 621; it is stored for the person and not for the browser, so it holds after a reload and on another machine, and an amber chip counting what is hidden — *1 batch set aside · show* — is the way back to it, with the run itself named in the menu that chip opens. The whole working set is in the address, so a link opens the same list on the same question — even one far down a batch of a thousand, the list opening at that question rather than at its top, with a line above its first row that brings in the questions above — and Back undoes one filter at a time. **Answer many at once.** Every row in the list is a change the source proposes, and a proposal has two answers — accept it or reject it — whatever the row asks. Each row carries a box; a click ticks it, a shift-click ticks the run of rows between it and the last one clicked, `x` ticks the row the keyboard is on and `Esc` clears every tick, and a box over the list ticks the whole page and then offers every row the filters match — *Select all 1 078 matching* — for the day a source's first run puts a thousand arrivals in front of a curator wanting the same answer. A bar at the foot of the page names the selection by kind — *40 arrivals, 3 refusals, 12 objects gone from the source* — and offers the two answers, each saying under it what it does for each kind in the words that kind's own card uses (*Keep it out* for an arrival, *Former — delisted, still there* for an object gone from the source; a refusal's put-back adds *until the next run*, since a batch's answer is not kept the way a card's is), and *Lost* only where every ticked row is an object or a place that has left, since that is the curator's own claim about the world and not an answer to the proposal. With more than one row ticked the right-hand side sums the selection — by question, by source, by run, and what each answer would do — instead of opening one card. A batch on screen within one page runs without asking, since the count is in view and every answer can be taken back; past that, and for all matching, the page asks once and names what it will do. Each object is answered on its own and recorded on its own, so the line afterwards says what refused and why, in groups; a batch that stops part-way says which rows may already be answered. The ticks are not in the address — a link must not replay someone's half-finished selection — and they are cleared, with a line saying so, when the list they were made against changes. **A refusal is a proposal to keep something out.** What a run proposes for a row its own rule turned down is the refusal, so accepting everything a run proposed keeps those rows out, and rejecting a refusal is what puts one back. A batch answer is about a selection, not about the one row, so it decides nothing for good. A refusal accepted in a batch stops being asked about, and a row put back in a batch is back until the next run, which applies the rule again and asks once more if the row still fails it. Only the refusal's own card — one row read, and a note on why — keeps a decision that the next run respects. That rule comes from a real batch on 14 September. A curator accepted a run's feed, and 119 rows the rules had refused came back and were fixed in place: the Egyptian Museum of Berlin as an art museum beside the Neues Museum that houses it, three concentration camps listed as public art, and *Fallen Astronaut*, which stands on the Moon. On 12 October a curator read those 119 as a list: eight were kept — the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum, the Louvre Abu Dhabi and six well-known monuments one Wikipedia edition short of the line — and the other 111 went back to their rule's verdict, each with a line in its own history saying which batch it undid. **And the two kinds that had only a yes now have a no.** An arrival nobody wants is *kept out*: it stays hidden, comes back from the kept-out list at the foot of the page exactly as a rule's refusal does, and the next run meets that decision rather than asking again. Unread points and works under a visible object can be *turned down*: they stay hidden and stop being asked about, and come back from the turned-down list at the foot of the page — and a turned-down point stops counting: an unread point counts its object into a region from the day it arrives, since most are about to be published, so a museum listed in a region only for a branch nobody wanted leaves that region's list when the branch is turned down. Where the point was replacing another, readers keep the old pin, and the source has to move the point somewhere else to ask again. Nothing on the screen recommends an answer: the two ways to settle a disagreement sit under the two versions they apply to, in the same order, and the marking that shows which words differ says the same thing about both columns rather than colouring one of them as the right one.
- **Open what the source says, from the id** — the id beside an object's name on a review card is how the source knows the object, and it used to only copy itself; now it opens the page that names it — the Wikidata item for a museum or a monument, the World Heritage Centre's own page for a site — with the copy one click away beside it, for the moments the id is the thing to quote. A work listed on a card opens twice: its name at the Wikidata item it was imported from, and *Wikipedia* beside it at the article Wikidata resolves for it, since almost every work has one: the reader's card links the article the works runs store, and the curator's lists resolve it at the click. A work a card holds a change to carries the link in its heading, beside its name and maker: the stored article where a run has read one (the Louvre's held rename of *The Coronation of Napoleon* opens its article), resolved at the click where no run has read it yet, and its Wikidata item where a run found no English article, so every work's heading has one way out to its source. The article link on an object's own card stays what the source stored, because the few museums without one have no English article to open.
- **See what the object is, on the card** — a review card's header says in a line what the object is, in its source's words: the Hermitage is "museum in Saint Petersburg, Russia", the Vatican Museums "museums of the Vatican City". The line is cut to fit and shown whole on hover, beside the picture, the coordinates, the links and the regions it counts in. A button that opened the object again under the card only repeated that header, so it is gone.
- **See whether an official website still answers** — a review card that shows a place's official website, or a proposal of a new one, says beside the link whether the site still answers. A dead one is marked *site unreachable*, with why, *domain not found*, *page not found (404)* or *domain for sale*, and when it was checked: Villa d'Este's proposed site has a domain that no longer exists, and Santa Chiara's answers with a page offering the domain for sale. A site that answers gets a small check mark, *site responds*, with the date it was checked, which says it answered and never that it is safe, since an expired domain bought by someone else answers too. A site that refused the check or did not answer in time is said not to have been checked, never to be dead. Every line names the site, so none reads as the card's own question having been answered. Where a proposal replaces a dead address with a live one, as Lourdes's does, the card shows the repair for what it is. Only the cards on screen are checked, and the curator still decides.
- **Choose between two sources that describe one place differently** — once a World Heritage site and the same place from Wikidata are one place, the two can disagree about what it is called, how it looks or where it stands: UNESCO calls it Rila Monastery and shows the church under snow, Wikidata calls it the Monastery of Saint John of Rila and shows it in summer. They can link different Wikipedia articles too: the World Heritage site of the Statue of Liberty is Liberty Island, while the Wikidata kinds link the statue's own article, and the card names both with a link to read each. One place of a serial World Heritage site that another kind also brings is asked about the same way: the World Heritage list calls one of Ancient Thebes' places the Temple of Karnak, Places of worship calls it the Karnak Temple Complex, and the curator picks which name visitors read. Neither source wins by running last. The place keeps what visitors already see, and the review page asks once per place: a row for each fact the two disagree on, both values side by side — each photograph with its photographer — the one visitors see marked and already chosen, and one button that says what it will change, from *Keep what readers see* to *Publish 2 changes*. Two points are shown on one map, and the row says how far apart they stand and which point a source gave only to the whole minute, a cell about two kilometres across. Each source's column links its own record of the place: the Wikidata entry a Wikidata kind reads, the site's page on the World Heritage list. For one place of a serial World Heritage site, the card names the site under the place's name — *Component 1209-002 of Agave Landscape and Ancient Industrial Facilities of Tequila*, linked to the site's page, where UNESCO maps where each of its places lies — and the World Heritage column says it is the list's record of that place, not of the whole site, so *Zona nucleo 02* is read as one place of the Tequila landscape and Archaeology's Guachimontones, 2.4 km away, is weighed against the right record. The facts the sources agree on, or that only one of them knows, are listed in a line rather than asked. A choice stands until one of the sources sends something new about that fact, and only then is the question asked again. Where the deployment uses Jev, the card also says which value Jev would pick and how sure it is — on Rila Monastery it picks UNESCO's name at 96 % — as a suggestion beside the values, never chosen for the curator; an admin sees in AI Settings what those suggestions cost and how often curators agreed.
- **Answer what a sync run could not decide** — a review page collects the decisions a run leaves open, and nothing else. An object the source stopped listing gets one of three answers: it was delisted but is still standing, it no longer exists, or the source simply hiccupped and nothing happened. The first two are different facts about the same object and can both be true — a destroyed site can remain inscribed, an intact one can be struck off — so they are answered separately rather than as one status. A place that is several kinds at once can be dropped by one kind's source alone — a reclassification makes the archaeology source stop finding the Capitoline Museums while the art-museum source still lists them — and then the curator is asked only whether it is still of that kind: a false alarm keeps it there, and "no longer this kind" takes it out of that kind's list while it stays in the others; a delisted World Heritage Site instead stays in the World Heritage list, marked **Former** there and only there, since having been one is worth knowing. Whether the place still stands is not asked while other sources still list it. A field where the source keeps proposing a value over a curator's edit shows both versions **in full, side by side, with the differing words marked** — no longer cut at a hundred and twenty characters, which is what made a two-hundred-character edit against five hundred characters of source text impossible to judge on the page that asked. **What a run proposes is a table of facts, and every fact speaks for itself.** A card says first what the run proposes — how many facts are new, how many changed, and which — and then lays them out one to a row: the fact's name, what readers see now, what the run proposes. The name is the fact's own, not the database's, and it carries its meaning: hover or tab onto *in danger* and the card says what the List of World Heritage in Danger is, what it means on the ground, and what a change to it usually means. A value looks the way readers see it — the In Danger badge is the badge, with the year the site was listed; the inscription criteria are the numerals with their meaning one hover away; a picture credit is the photographer and the terms; a coordinate is a coordinate; nothing is a dash. And under a value that changed, one line says what *this* change means to someone who has stood there: "Listed since 2003 — readers see no badge today; publishing adds it", "Moved 33 km north — may fall in a different region; check the pin", "Spelling only — the country is the same", "Inscription years do not change — check the source page". The source's extra data about an object used to arrive as the whole bag printed twice, side by side, for the curator to find the difference by eye — the whole of what a run proposed about the Bamiyan Valley, that its inscription criteria had arrived and a picture credit with them, was asked as eight named things on one side against six on the other. It is three rows now, and the two that arrive are marked as arriving. Two things a curator used to be asked about are not on the card at all: the machine's own labels, which nothing on the site reads and which repeated the criteria row beside them, and the storage the facts live in, which was never a fact. On a conflict card each fact is answered in its own column, and the two whole-card answers stay for the common case. **A change inside the object will read on the same card.** A run already records that a place of a serial site was renamed or a work's attribution corrected; when the card carries those, each part is its own group of rows under the object's, headed by the place's or the work's name with a way to open it — the table is built for that, and the object is only its first group. Around them the card now says what the decision rests on: the object itself, with its picture, its place on the map, the source's own page, and the regions it falls in; and per field, who wrote the version that stands, when the run that disagrees finished, and what anyone answered about that same field before — so the page stops calling another curator's work "my edit". Each field can be answered on its own, because a run improves and damages in the same breath, and taking a better description used to mean taking a mangled name with it; **Both answers are answers now.** Accepting the source's value lifts the protection on that field, which is what applies it and takes the question off the page. Keeping the curator's own used to be the absence of an action: visitors went on seeing their version, and the identical card came back after every single run — one object's arrived three times in two days with the source proposing the same text each time. Now it is a button, and it settles the question the same way accepting does. What it settles is narrow on purpose: the answer is about *that text*, so a source that later proposes something different is heard again, while one that keeps repeating itself is not. The two answers are not mirror images about what visitors see, and it is worth being exact: accepting puts the source's text on the site — right away for most fields, at the next sync for a moved location or a metadata key — while refusing changes nothing anyone can see, because the curator's version was already the one on display. So refusing is only ever a decision about whether the disagreement stays open. A curator can leave the card alone as before, and the page no longer pretends that is the only way to stand by their own words. A few kinds of disagreement — a moved location, a metadata key — cannot be applied on the spot; accepting those lifts the curator's protection and the next sync brings the value in, which the page says plainly rather than leaving the curator to guess when it takes effect. There is one exception, and the page says so instead of hiding it: when a kind's own rule turns a row down — an archaeological museum in a list of art museums, a stretch of painted wall, a cathedral among public art because Spain's heritage register calls it a monument, a Pietà that hangs inside St Peter's — the row is already hidden from visitors, because a candidate that fails the same rule is never added in the first place and one that predates the rule has to end up in the same place. That is not a guess about the world, so it is not offered as one: the card states the rule's own objection and asks a different question — was the rule right? A rule is a kind's, so it hides the row from that kind's list only: when the archaeology rule refuses the Capitoline Museums, they stay an art museum on every map, and nobody is asked, since the refusal changes nothing a traveller finds. The card comes once no other kind offers the place, when the refusal decides whether it stays in the catalogue at all, as for Amber Fort or the Kelpies; a place two kinds' rules refused is one card with a section for each, answered one kind at a time. **The objection is stated in ordinary words, and the evidence behind it can be looked at.** What a rule records for itself — "painting-share: 2 painting(s) vs 9 sculptural work(s)" — names internal tests, states no threshold and never says where its numbers came from; read once it is opaque, and a curator reads nineteen of them at a sitting. So the card says what was found in one sentence, a question mark beside it holds how the rule decides and how much it takes to pass, and the number in the sentence is the thing to hover: it opens the works themselves, with their pictures, their makers and their dates. "None of the 5 famous works is a painting" is a claim a curator cannot check; five named sculptures they can see is a question they can answer. Where the sentence names a single work instead of counting — the painting that pulled a cathedral into a list of museums — that work is shown first and whatever else the place holds is listed under it, so a phrase naming one thing never opens an unlabelled list of three. Either answer sticks against the machine, and no later run reverses it. Keeping a row out does not stick against the person who did it: because a kept-out row is hidden from its kind's list — and, where the place is in no other kind, from every list and its own address — the page keeps its own kept-out list, collapsed at the foot, a card for each kind a place was kept out of, where one click puts any of them back. Apart from that, until someone answers, none of it has changed anything visitors see
- **Be asked only about what a visitor would notice** — a source whose changes wait for a person held every rewrite of a place readers already see, and half of what it held changed nothing a visitor reads: a component's name with its village added (*Church of All Saints* becoming *Church of All Saints, Blizne* among the Wooden Churches of Southern Małopolska), a photograph's credit re-read for the same file (*No machine-readable author provided. Ansgar Walk* becoming *Ansgar Walk*), an area re-measured by less than a tenth, and the Wikidata item of a World Heritage site that a Wikidata kind also admits, which every run of Places of worship and Archaeology asked about Ajanta Caves although no visitor ever sees it. Those now go straight through and are recorded with why nobody was asked, and so does a place's first subtitle, the one line under its name, where it had none: Wikidata's "2nd century BCE to 1st century CE Buddhist cave monuments located in Maharashtra, India" for Ajanta Caves, whose UNESCO paragraph is its description; it replaces nothing a visitor reads. So does a photograph's first credit, where the place already shows the picture with nobody named: Stonehenge's *Stonehenge Total.jpg* now names Stefan Kühn without a card. A type its kind has no word for is replaced the same way by one it has: when Public Art & Monuments split in two, Nelson's Column, left a *sculpture* under Monuments & memorials, became a *column* on that kind's next run, and the Arc de Triomphe an *arch*, without 98 cards for a curator. Until that run those places show no type. Nor is a curator asked about a fact no visitor is shown — a place's names in other languages, its area, a monument's year and makers — or about a text that changed only in its typesetting: Gondwana Rainforests of Australia's description with *Australia’s* written *Australia's*, or a component the World Heritage list once named *Gwangneung Cluster\t* and now names *Gwangneung Cluster*. A space put inside a word (*Hollokö* written *Hollok ö*) is a corruption and is still asked. A place's name starts with a capital, as English writes it, whatever style a source keeps: *Place de la Concorde* and *Mortuary temple of Hatshepsut*, while *al-Azhar Mosque* and *iSimangaliso Wetland Park* keep the spelling their own first word carries; the run gives the capital, and nobody is asked about it. Nor is a curator asked to remove what one kind of the catalogue never reads: a monument's year comes from the kinds that read it off Wikidata, so Charminar under Places of worship keeps its 1591 rather than being proposed to lose it on every pass. Once a visitor's screen starts to show one of those facts, the values written meanwhile come back to a curator once. A pin moved a short way is not asked about either: the Choragic Monument of Lysicrates moving 28 m onto the monument, or the Kröller-Müller Museum 41 m from its gift shop onto the building, takes the new spot at once. A move of 250 m or more, one that lands nearer another place of the catalogue (Reims Cathedral beside the Reims World Heritage property), or one into another district still waits for a curator, and so does a disagreement between two sources whose points stand 250 m or more apart. A name replaced, a subtitle replaced, a type moved to another of its kind's words, a different photograph, a changed licence or one Wikidata item replaced by another still waits on a curator.
- **See what a gated source is holding back** — three more kinds of open question surface on the same review page, for a source held back until a person looks (see "Only what a person has passed" above). An **arrival** is a whole new object nobody has passed yet — a museum, a site, an artwork — surfaced here with everything on it, because a curator's own screen is where the gate is allowed to open even before anyone has answered for the object. Where the deployment uses Jev, an arrival warns when Jev suspects the card is wrong — a part of a larger place, an object kept elsewhere, little or nothing left, not one place at all, or a place visited for something its kind does not offer — and says how sure it is: the Lupanar, which a travel guide and photographs taken there vouch for, is read as a part of a larger place, one house of Pompeii, and the Arch of Reunification, demolished in 2024, as nothing left. Where Jev agrees with the card it says nothing, and a World Heritage site is never asked, since its inscription already answers. It is a suggestion under the run's own question, never chosen for the curator, and an admin sees in AI Settings what those readings cost and how often curators answered as Jev read. A **held proposal** is the opposite shape: an object visitors already see, where the source wants to change something about it and the change is being kept off that screen until someone looks. It is not lost — the version visitors see stays exactly as it is. When what the source wants to change is the picture, the card draws both pictures — the one visitors see and the one proposed, each with its own photographer's name and licence under it and the file's name as the link to where it lives — rather than two web addresses to open in other tabs; a picture the product may not draw, or one that fails to load, shows no frame and no credit. A picture and its photographer's name are one question with one answer — the old photograph cannot be kept under the new name — so the card shows them as one row; a credit has a row of its own only where the picture stays and Wikimedia Commons has corrected whose it is. When what the source wants to change is where the object stands, the card opens the move on a map: the pin visitors see, the proposed one, and an arrow from the first to the second, close enough to tell whether 158 metres moved the pin onto the ruins or off them. Saying yes to that move is one act: publishing the coordinate moves the pin on the map with it, rather than leaving the object at its new position under its old pin. That holds for the places and works an object is made of, not only for the object itself: when a source moves a painting's attribution — *The Wine Glass* in the Gemäldegalerie, from Johannes Vermeer to an obscure namesake, which is what Wikidata did on 21 August — or corrects the name of one castle of a serial site, the change waits on the same card, grouped under the work's or the place's own name with both versions side by side and a way to open it: the castle on a map, the painting with its picture, its maker and the page it came from. Until then visitors go on seeing the attribution they saw yesterday. Before this, such a change landed the moment the run found it, with nobody asked — and of the twenty-two attributions that reached the site that way, the ones checked by hand were right, wrong and disputed in about equal measure. The third kind is what a visible object has quietly gained: a museum holding newly-arrived paintings, a site newly-arrived locations. The card gives both the number and the things themselves — the points by name with their coordinates, the works by title and maker — capped at a page's worth, because nobody reads seven hundred rows and nobody can answer for twelve works they cannot see either. And it says what each of them is, because "new" is often the wrong word: a point the source moved waits beside the pin it replaces and is called a point that moved, with how far, rather than a second place — and publishing it is the whole answer: the old spot is recorded as moved, on your publish, and never comes back asking what became of a place you just moved. A second source's point within ten metres of a pin visitors already see is that pin, never a point to read, and a proposed coordinate that close is said to be no move at all; a work visitors already see in one museum that arrives in another's list is said to be on show there already. The form that corrects a work names the museums the correction reaches, and says so when two of them are one museum the catalogue lists under two kinds. All three sit under one heading — "Waiting to be published" — because they share one sentence: none of it has reached a visitor. **An object is asked about once, however many of the three are true of it.** A museum whose label is being held back *and* which gained twelve paintings is one card with one heading and a row for each thing that is open — the label with both versions side by side, the new points listed under their count, the new works under theirs — because that is one object and one decision to the person looking at it, whatever it took to work that out. A place that is two kinds at once can be asked about by both — an archaeology source has just brought the Capitoline Museums, while their art-museum listing is holding a new picture — and it is still one place in the list, whose line names both kinds and says it has two questions; its card shows the place once and a section for each kind, headed with that kind's name, and each section's buttons answer for that kind alone, so publishing the archaeology arrival does not publish the art-museum picture; the archaeology section says that visitors already see the museum as an art museum and that publishing adds it to the archaeology list, rather than calling it something nobody can see; and a curator who looks after archaeology is the one asked about the archaeology arrival. What both kinds' runs propose alike about the place itself is asked once, not once per kind: the Vatican Museums' art-museum and archaeology runs both propose AD 120 for the Apollo Belvedere's year and the same website, and the card asks each once, under *This place*, naming both runs, above whatever each kind still asks on its own. One answer settles it for both. Two runs that propose different values are still asked in each kind's section, and so is a place's type within its kind, which belongs to that kind. The one question that stays separate is a disagreement about the curator's own wording: that is already decided, by them, and keeps its own card, so the same museum can appear twice with two different questions rather than once with two answers merged into a button. And a curator can look at the thing itself before deciding — one click on the card opens what a gated object actually holds, which is the only place an unpublished arrival can be seen at all, since it appears in no list, no count and on no map
- **Answer each thing on its own** — a museum can be waiting on two things at once: a sentence its source wants to rewrite, and twelve paintings that arrived since anyone looked. Those used to be one act, so a curator who did not want the new sentence kept the paintings invisible by refusing it — one doubtful field freezing twelve checked works. The card now asks in two tables laid out alike: what readers see that the source wants to change, and what has arrived that no reader has been shown yet. Every row of either carries its own *publish this* and *not this*, so one doubtful painting no longer holds back the eleven beside it, and one painting a curator does not want no longer needs a no that would take the eleven with it; where five or more have arrived, *turn all down* is one click — offered wherever it can turn down exactly the rows it counts, so not on a long list beside a moved point, which is asked with the changes rather than counted here. Each arrived work says whether it is new to the catalogue or already on show in another museum — Boy with Thorn, on show at the Capitoline Museums under Art Museums, arriving at the same museum under Archaeology, is not a new work — and, where Wikidata names no museum for it and only English Wikipedia's collection shelf puts it there, says so, so the Dead Sea Scrolls arrive at the Israel Museum and the Code of Hammurabi at the Louvre by Wikipedia's word and not Wikidata's, and a curator can weigh that word — and shows its picture, its maker and year, its Wikipedia article and how many Wikipedias write about it; its name opens it, the one way in, where it can be corrected. A point the source moved is not an arrival but a change to what readers see, so it sits with the changes, headed by its place, with the distance and the map of the move. Saying no to a move keeps the pin where readers see it, and the source has to move it somewhere else to ask again. Where the change is the object's own coordinate, the point that is that move goes with it either way: published, the pin moves with the coordinate; refused, both stay where they were — one question, asked once. The things waiting are listed rather than counted, the works most famous first, so a decision about twelve paintings is made by looking at twelve paintings. Where there are more than a page of them — a serial site can hold hundreds — the card lists the first twenty-five and says plainly how many there are, rather than letting a short list stand for a long one. One button publishes everything on the card for a curator who has looked and agrees with all of it. A place that is two kinds at once — the Capitoline Museums as an art museum and as an archaeological collection — asks each kind's arrivals in that kind's own section: the paintings the art-museum run brought under Art Museums, the finds the archaeology run brought under Archaeology, each for the curator who holds that source, and a yes or a no in one section leaves the other's rows where they are.
- **Answer one fact at a time, and say no to one without claiming it** — a run improves and damages in the same breath. Run 68 wants six things from *Getbol, Korean Tidal Flats (Phase II)*: to drop "(Phase II)" from the name visitors read, to rewrite the description for the 2026 extension, to drop it from the site's six local names as well, to change the photograph, and two more nobody sees. Until now the card's buttons were all of it or none of it, so a curator who doubted the rename either lost the better description or took the rename with it. Each fact now carries its own two answers, in its own column, beside the two versions it is about — the same shape the disagreement card has had, because it is the same decision. Publishing one leaves the others waiting, and the card stays with them on it rather than vanishing half-answered. **And "no" is finally a thing a curator can say.** Before this the only way to refuse one proposed value was to write your own over it, which claims the field — a statement about whose words those are, not about what is wrong with these, and one that outlives the question: the source's *next* proposal for that field, possibly the right one, would meet the claim and nobody would ever be asked. Refusing writes nothing, so on its own it changes nothing visitors see: what they see had already won every run since the gate first held it, and what a refusal changes is the asking. And it is narrow on purpose — the answer is about *that value*, so a source that comes back with something different is heard again, while one repeating itself is not. That holds for the places and works an object is made of too: the photograph of *The Wine Glass* can be taken while its attribution to an obscure namesake is refused, and each answer names the work it is about. **A fact is a fact whatever shelf it is kept on.** Which of a run's proposals could be answered on its own used to depend on how the catalogue happens to store them: a site's danger listing had its own two buttons, while its inscription criteria and its picture credit shared a pair, because those two live in one place in the database and nothing else. That is not a distinction a curator has any reason to see, and on 1227 of the 1484 cards holding such a change it meant taking a photographer's name unchecked in order to take UNESCO's own criteria. The site's own name in six languages was the same thing one shelf over: the card listed all six and offered one pair of buttons across them, so a curator who could read the Korean and wanted it corrected had to take the Arabic, the Chinese, the Spanish, the French and the Russian on trust, or refuse the lot — and the only thing on the screen saying so was a line under the buttons counting how many they answered. Now each of them is asked separately, and the run records them that way. Cards raised before this keep the shape they were raised with — what a run recorded is what happened, and rewriting it would quietly cancel answers already given — so they change as each is proposed afresh. One thing is deliberately not answerable on its own: a photograph and the credit that belongs to it move together, because a picture shown under the previous photographer's name is a false statement about a real person — take the picture and its credit comes with it, keep the picture and the credit stays as it is. That now holds for a site as it always did for a work, which closes the one gap where refusing could reach a visitor: on an older card, refusing a site's source data and then publishing its new photograph sends the picture out with nobody credited, since the refused credit may not be written and the old one describes a photograph nobody is shown any more. The catalogue's own checks say so when it happens, so somebody can go and find the photographer. Neither button is the coloured one: the card's premise is that visitors keep what they can see until somebody says otherwise, and making one answer look preferred would be the screen recommending it
- **Say that visitors may see it** — the answer to all three of those, and the only way anything a gated source wrote reaches a visitor. Publishing an arrival takes the whole object: it appears in lists, on the map and in counts, and the points and works that arrived with it appear with it, because nobody wants a museum published with its paintings still held back. Publishing a held proposal applies what the source wanted to change and takes the question off the page — the object's own fields and those of its places and works alike, a picture together with the credit that belongs to it, and the line afterwards names each part it touched: "The Wine Glass: artist applied". A part whose field the curator has meanwhile corrected by hand keeps their version, as the object does; a place the source has since stopped listing is left alone and said so, since there is nothing left to write to. And a visible object holding newly-arrived contents can have those published on their own, without re-answering for the object — three checked paintings do not make the museum re-read. A curator can publish without settling a separate disagreement about their own edit: whether visitors may see this and whose wording it is are different questions, so a field the curator has claimed keeps their version and the rest of the object still goes out. **That is also how to take everything but one thing**: a curator who wants the twelve new paintings and not the new label writes the label their own way first — editing a field claims it — and then publishes; their wording stays, everything else lands, and the page says which fields it left alone. The card says so where the proposed wording is shown, because an escape route nobody is told about is not one. What a curator was looking at is checked against what is stored before anything is written, so a card drawn before another run landed is refused with what the object says now rather than publishing a proposal nobody read. One thing publishing will not do is settle two questions at once: an object its own kind turned down cannot be published until that refusal is answered, and the page says so rather than quietly making it visible — putting it back is what publishes it, in one step, so nobody is asked the same thing twice. That step only does something if nobody had looked at the object yet: for one visitors could already see before its kind refused it, putting it back is just the admission answer, since the refusal was the only thing that had hidden it. Publishing an already-visible object is harmless and repeatable — it changes nothing that was already decided, which is what makes it the way to take newly-arrived contents under a row that visitors can already see. Publishing a point that replaces another does one thing more, and it is the point of the click: the pin it replaces stops being shown at the same moment, so nobody ever sees the same place twice, and the regions the object counts towards are recomputed for the point that is now the real one. **Every publication says what it did**, in one line above the queue: which wording landed, how many points and works became visible, and — the two nothing else would ever record — the moment a replaced pin stopped being shown, and whether recomputing the object's regions failed afterward, so a publication that landed with stale regions is never read as an unqualified success. **That last one names the world views it left stale**, because rebuilding them is an admin's job and not the curator's: the one thing a curator can do is tell an admin which object and which world view, so the line says exactly that instead of leaving them to report that something about regions went wrong somewhere. "Put it back" on a refusal says the same way whether the row is now visible or whether it only answered the refusal, rather than leaving a curator looking for a second button. And a card answered by someone else in the meantime says what the server now holds and stays live rather than going dead: the page redraws itself against the newer state instead of leaving a button that can only fail
- **Answer for one place inside an object** — an object is often made of several places: a serial World Heritage site of seven housing estates, a museum with one address and two hundred works. When a run stops offering *one of those places*, visitors lose that pin straight away — sometimes one they had ticked — and until now nobody was asked about it and no screen said it had happened. The review page now carries that question too, one row per object and one answer per place, and it is placed above the refusals for that reason: a refusal never showed the row at all, while this has already taken something away. Every card says what the object is made of, which is the piece that was missing everywhere: a proposed description of one estate of seven used to sit against the whole site's text with nothing to say the site had parts. And each lost place carries the two facts that make the answer decidable rather than a guess — whether anyone had been there, and how far away the source now lists that same part. The distance matters more than it sounds: the first real case in the catalogue turned out to be **one centimetre**, a coordinate rewritten at finer precision, and a page that called that "moved" would have been wrong about the only thing it was for. A run no longer asks that question about a rewrite: ten metres or less is read as the same place written more exactly, and no card is raised for it. A few such questions are still reachable by other routes, and the card says so itself rather than pretending otherwise. The ones already recorded against a place somebody had visited are left exactly as they are, because moving a person's record of having been somewhere is a decision for a curator and not for a repair script, so those still come round to be answered. And where a source publishes two parts of one component a stone's throw apart — nothing in the catalogue does, and it would be the source disagreeing with itself about what one part is — the run can still ask once about a part that never went anywhere. Ten metres is chosen to be smaller than the things being pointed at, so the opposite still reaches a curator: a source that re-centres a national park by two kilometres, or moves a museum's pin across the square to a new entrance, has made a judgement about a place, and that is theirs to confirm. The three answers are the same three an object gets — the source dropped it, it no longer exists, or the source was wrong — and two of them change nothing anyone sees, because the place was already off the map the moment the run noticed. Only "false alarm" puts it back. Nothing is ever deleted: whoever had been there keeps that record whatever is answered
- **A renumbered component is not a move** — when UNESCO extends a property, every component's reference changes (`829bis-001` → `829ter-001` for Pompeii) and nothing else does. The run keeps the row: the same place on the map, the same picture and description, the same curator's words, and the same "I was here" — no card asks a curator to publish a move of nothing. A component that an extension both renumbered and moved is still a move, held for a curator as before.
- **Take a verdict back** — a mis-click is one click away from hiding an object from everyone, so it has to be one click away from coming back. An arrival a curator kept out by hand comes back the same way a rule's refusal does — *Put it back* in the kept-out list — and publishes as it always has. Open the item and it offers exactly that: "It is still listed" for something recorded as delisted, right where the item already sits — those are never hidden — and "It does still exist" for something recorded as gone, which first has to be revealed, since hiding it is what the verdict did. For those two the review queue cannot host it — the queue lists open questions, and an answered one has left it — which is why it lives where the object is still reachable. Three things have nowhere else to live, and the queue keeps them all after all, out of the way of the work but one click from coming back: a row a rule kept out, a **place an object lost that somebody has already answered about**, and a **point or work somebody turned down**. A place is not something you can open on its own — it is a pin on a map, and both answers take the pin off the map — so once the question is answered it is on no screen at all, and the answer could not be undone. Now it is listed: what was decided, when, by whom, and the note they left. Taking an answer back makes the person taking it the author of where the place now stands, so the note goes with the answer it explained rather than being reattributed to somebody who never wrote it — and the object's own History is where the wording stays readable, which is a promise the History had to be taught to keep: a verdict on a place used to appear there as a machine word with nothing under it. Each answer is taken back on its own, because they are two different claims — that the source stopped listing the place, and that the place is gone from the world — and the pin comes back only once neither is still holding it, which the screen says before the click rather than after. What it will not pretend is that taking an answer back settles anything with the source: if the source still does not offer the place, the next run will notice and ask again. The turned-down points and works are the third of those lists and the plainest: a part nobody passed was never on a reader's screen, and turning it down took it off the curator's too, so until this list existed a mis-click could be found only in the object's history and undone nowhere. Each is listed under the object that holds them, with who turned it down and when, and the note they left; asking about one again puts it back on that object's contents card and nothing more — publishing it is still what shows it to anyone, which the screen says beside the button rather than after the click. A point counts toward its regions again from that moment, since an unread point counts its object into a region and a turned-down one does not. Two rows on that list get a different sentence, and get it before the click rather than after: one whose part the source has stopped listing since it was turned down, where asking again puts the question back on the record and nothing else follows, because nothing shows a part the source no longer offers; and one under an object with a question of its own — nobody has passed it, a rule kept it out, or the source has dropped it — where the take-back is refused outright, so the button is not offered and the row names the question to answer first. A point that moved comes back as the same move, beside the pin it would replace, which readers have kept seeing all along. Paging through one of those answered lists leaves the questions already loaded above it where they were, and an open list says which of the curator's filters — the region, the run, the kind of question — do not narrow it, since only the search and the source do.
- **Be asked again when a run stops agreeing with your pinned answer** — a curator who puts a place back over a rule, or keeps one out, pins that answer, and every run leaves the place as they left it. The pin now remembers what it answered, and a run that says something else asks instead of staying silent: the review page shows a card under *A run asks again about an answer you pinned*, with the answer, its date and note, the rule's verdict of the day — the objection a put-back overrode, or the refusal a keep-out agreed with, never an objection the rule did not raise — and what the run says now. A note the system wrote about how an answer came about is said to be the system's, apart from the curator's own words, wherever the answer is shown: this card, the pins list, the kept-out list and the place's history. A museum a curator put back into Art Museums over the rule of the day ("probe: site class villa") is asked again when a later run refuses it for a reason that answer never saw ("a villa, not a museum — it reached the list because Galatea is kept there"). Two answers: keep it, and the pin answers the new reason until a run says something else again; or let the rule decide, and the run's verdict is written at once. Nothing is asked when only a count in the reason moved ("17 sitelinks" to "19"), when the rule has come round to the curator's answer, or when a place kept out is still kept out for a reworded reason. Nothing changes for visitors until someone answers, and a batch answer never answers this card: a pin is one person's answer to one place.
- **See every answer you pinned, and hand one back** — a place put back into a kind, or kept out of it, against the kind's rule stays as a curator left it on every run; until now nothing showed which places those were. The place's curation dialog now says, per kind, that its answer is pinned: who gave it, when, the rule's objection it answered and the note — Villa Farnesina's "put it back in Art Museums on 8 August" over "a villa, not a museum". The review page lists every such answer in a curator's scope, collapsed at its foot under each kind, until curators have a home screen of their own. *Hand the pin back* gives the place to the rule again: nothing moves at once, and the next run decides — a place put back stays until a run still turns it down, which then asks afresh; a place kept out stays out until the rule would take it in. A place a curator kept out on arrival answered no rule, so it is not handed back: the list says to put it back from the kept-out list, which is its way back, since a source that publishes a list, such as the World Heritage list, never takes a refused place back by itself. After a kind's rule changes, an admin can put every such answer to a curator again: *Asking again about pins*, one of the ways to run the sync behind *Start Sync*'s arrow on the source's card, runs the source and asks about each pinned place the rule still disagrees with — the ones answered on purpose as well — without releasing any, and its closing line says how many it asked about and how many it agrees with. Such a card says so plainly: the run was asked to look again and still says what the curator answered over, rather than claiming the rule has changed its words.
- **Be asked about an offered place with nothing to see** — a kind's own line lets in a few places a traveller would find nothing at: Eboracum, Roman York under the living city; the Arch of Reunification, demolished in 2024; the Venus of Tan-Tan, a stone kept in a museum. Every run of Art Museums, Public art, Monuments & memorials, Places of worship and Archaeology now looks at the places it offers and asks a curator about the ones where both signs agree: fewer than two of four witnesses say a visitor goes there — a travel guide, photographs taken there, a heritage listing or visitor facts on Wikidata, something standing on the map — and Jev reads the place, sure enough, as having nothing to see, as not one place, or as an object kept elsewhere. Saqqara, which the sources miss but Jev reads as a site, is not asked. The review page shows a card under *Offered places with nothing to see — refuse them?*: which witnesses vouched and which did not, Jev's reading and how sure it was, and the suggestion, refuse it from the kind. Refusing takes the place off that kind's list for visitors at once, no run brings it back, and the kept-out list puts it back with one click; keeping it closes the question, and it is not asked again. Nothing is refused until a curator says so. A selection from the list can be kept at once; refusing is done card by card, since a refusal stands against the kind's own rule for good, and an admin sees in AI Settings how often curators agreed with Jev here, with its other readings.
- **Be asked about a work the world reads but few Wikipedias describe** — Public art admits a sculpture by how many language editions of Wikipedia have an article about it, and some works every visitor to a country seeks out have too few: the Kelpies by Falkirk, Louise Bourgeois's *Maman* outside the Guggenheim Bilbao. Public art's run now also reads how often each work's articles were read over the last year in every edition the catalogue counts, and a work read as much as the median of the works the kind already holds arrives anyway, waiting for a curator rather than shown at once; the door is open only while Public art's arrivals wait for a curator, and a source an admin publishes without one admits nothing through it. It still has to be public art by the kind's other rules: a statue inside a basilica is refused whatever its readers. Its card in the review queue says how many times it was read and against what bar, whether the year's reading was one burst, and asks whether a visitor can see it there now. A work let in this way stays while it is read above the lower bar, the 35th percentile of the same works, and the run's log names every work the door admitted. An art museum is let in the same way, through a painting or a sculpture it holds, and the bar there is lower: a work read more than the least-read eighth of the works at the line admits its museum. That brings the Thyssen-Bornemisza, the Albertina, the Guggenheims, SFMOMA and the Barnes Foundation, whose famous pictures are read worldwide but described in fewer than 22 languages. A museum still never enters for its own fame. Archaeology reads a guide's word the same way: a dig a Wikivoyage guide names comes in at that lower bar too, for a curator, because a dig is often overshadowed by its town or by the monument beside it. The Roman Agora of Athens, one of the seven sites of the city's combined ticket, is read less than the Tower of the Winds that stands inside it; the Sanctuary of Asclepius at Epidaurus and the Pyramids of Meroë come in the same way. And an archaeology museum is let in for a find read as widely, at the same lower bar: the Gansu Provincial Museum arrives for the Flying Horse, read worldwide but described in ten languages, and Salisbury's museum for the Amesbury Archer, each for a curator with its find's reading. Such a find is marked must-see wherever it is shown — the Boxer at Rest in Rome, the Sutton Hoo helmet in the British Museum. Fame is not enough for something no archaeologist dug up: the London Hammer, a favourite of creationists, and a painted Japanese handscroll are never taken for finds. Monuments & memorials asks the guide alone: a monument described in fewer than 22 languages that a Wikivoyage guide names comes in for a curator, with no readers' bar — under this kind's line readers bring tombs and historic sites filed as monuments, while the guide brings what a traveller actually seeks out: the Voortrekker Monument, the Scott Monument, the Kigali Genocide Memorial, the National World War II Memorial, the Shrine of Remembrance. Its card says how many languages describe it and that a guide names it, and asks whether it is a monument a visitor goes to see. And a memorial Wikidata also types an archive, a research institute, a government body, a hall of fame, a sculpture garden or a mausoleum is now the memorial first: Yad Vashem, the Australian War Memorial, Nagasaki Peace Park, the Walhalla, Anıtkabir and the Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum are offered where before the other typing kept them out. Towers reads the guide with a year of readers: a tower a Wikivoyage guide names that is read 15,000 times a year or more comes in for a curator however few languages describe it — Bologna's Asinelli and Garisenda towers, the Dom Tower of Utrecht, the Torrazzo of Cremona and the Martinitoren are each described in fewer than fifteen, and climbed by everyone who visits their towns; the card says how many languages describe it, how often it is read and that a guide names it. Public art reads two marks the same way: a work a Wikivoyage guide names or that Atlas Obscura lists, read 20,000 times a year or more, comes in for a curator however few languages describe it — Cadillac Ranch, the Crown Fountain, Salvation Mountain, Spiral Jetty, Carhenge, the Headington Shark — with a card that names the marks it carries and how often it is read. A work on a museum's or a church's grounds is still that venue's, however marked: Spoonbridge and Cherry stays the Walker Art Center's. Art Museums, in turn, no longer turns away a museum because Wikidata also calls it a collection — the Frick Collection and Palazzo Vecchio arrive with the works that name them — and a work that names a museum network is placed at the house the network's own address points to: *The Lady of Shalott*, which Wikidata hands to the Tate and to the National Gallery that once owned it, hangs at Tate Britain, where before the network's name kept it on Trafalgar Square.
- **Take a place out of one of its kinds from its own card** — a place a run never asked about can still be one a visitor misses nothing by: the Theatre of Pompey, whose curve survives only in the street plan of Campo de' Fiori, or the podium that is all that is left of the Temple of Concord. The place's curation dialog opens on *Kinds*: one row per kind the place belongs to, with its type and where it stands — offered, waiting for a curator, or kept out by its rule. An offered kind can be taken out: one step says what readers lose (that kind's lists, counts and map at once, while the place stays in its other kinds; or, where it is the only kind, every list), a note says why, and from then on no run brings it back. A kept-out kind says who kept it out, when and why, and *Put it back* returns it, as the kept-out list on the review page does. An arrival and a rule's refusal are answered on the review page, and their rows say so.
- **Add a place to a kind no run offers it in** — a run finds a place only through its kind's rule, and some places fall between the rules: Haus Schwarzenberg in Berlin is a courtyard covered in street art, but nothing English Wikipedia or Wikidata files under street art names it, so no run of Public art finds it. At the foot of *Kinds*, *Add to a kind…* opens a short panel: the kind (only those the place is not in yet, never World Heritage Sites, which only UNESCO's inscription makes), its type where the kind has types, what the add does — readers find the place in that kind's lists, map and counts from now on, and later runs keep it there though they never find it themselves — and an optional note kept in the place's history. The added kind's row then says who added it, when and why, and *Take out…* undoes it.
- **Choose whether a put-back is remembered** — putting a place back over its rule used to pin it without saying so: the button read "The rule was wrong — put it back", and nothing on the card said that every later run would leave the place alone. The refusal card now carries *Remember a put-back on later runs*, ticked, with one sentence for what it means — later runs keep the place while the rule turns it down for the same reason, and a new reason asks again. Unticked, the place is back for now and the next run applies the rule again, asking afresh if it still turns it down: the answer for a curator who means "show it, and let the next run look again". The place's history says which of the two was chosen. A batch answer offers no such choice and pins nothing.
- **View curation history** — every action (edit, reject, unreject, create, assign, remove, accepting a source value, refusing one over your own edit, refusing one a gated source was holding, publishing, and the lifecycle verdicts above) is logged with who did what and when, viewable per experience. A curator who never set a display name, or whose name is only spaces, is shown as “A curator” rather than as a blank beside the act, and the review page's turned-down parts and withdrawn points say “a curator” the same way. Each entry says what the act was in the words the product uses for it — “kept out”, “put back”, “took the source’s” — rather than the machine's name for the column, and shows what the row holds that the name cannot: the fields a publication applied and the ones it left as the curator wrote them, the value taken from the source and the one waiting for the next sync, the pin handed back with a coordinate, the picture credit dropped with a picture — and, where an edit replaced or removed a picture, the photographer's name and licence on each side of the change, in the words the card uses, rather than a machine's `[object Object]`. **A note a curator typed is on that screen at last.** Two cards ask for one — the object a run stopped finding, and the row a rule kept out — and each says on the field that it will be kept in this object's curation history; it was kept and shown nowhere, so the sentence explaining why something was marked gone, or why a refusal was overruled, existed only in the database. Refusing is in that list for a reason worth stating: standing by your own words used to leave no trace at all, so the history could show a field accepted from the source three times and say nothing about the times someone declined it. **A merge is in the history too, and can be taken back there.** When two entries that were one place are made one, the place that stayed says which entry went into it and which kinds came with it — “Pantheon (#14749) merged into Pantheon (#13198), bringing Archaeology” — and, where the catalogue did it because both entries name one Wikidata item, says the catalogue did it rather than naming a curator. A curator who finds the two were not one place after all presses *Undo this merge* on that row: the other place is its own again, with its kinds, its points and its works, and every traveller's visit is as it was unless they have written to it since. Two merges into one place are taken back latest first, and the row says so when asked out of turn. What was decided about the entry that went in comes with it: the place's history shows those entries too, each marked as being about the entry since merged in — so the Tower of Hercules, which holds the public-art membership its second entry brought, shows the decision that pinned it on that entry, where before only the merge was there. The history a region-scoped curator sees is bounded by that scope: an experience assigned to several regions shows only the entries for the regions they cover, plus entries that name no region. Global and source curators, and admins, see the whole history
- **Say what an identifier is to a place** — a place's curation dialog lists what the sources know it by: its Wikidata item, its World Heritage reference, each linked to the page that names it, with whether it identifies the place or only refers to it, and the other places that hold the same one. A curator who finds that an item names another thing — a chapel carrying its monastery's item — says so there: it only refers to this place, or it is taken off, with a note, and no run writes it back; one the sources missed is named there, and the history records each word. Two places that turn out to be one thing are not corrected here but merged, and the dialog says which other place holds the identifier so that is the obvious next step.

Curators are the quality layer. They're local experts or subject-matter specialists who keep the content accurate and relevant. The system trusts them — nobody approves a curator's work, and there are no drafts. The review page runs the other way round: it is the machine asking a curator, not a curator waiting on anyone. Actions are logged for accountability, and curator edits are protected from being overwritten by automated syncs.

### Admin

Everything a curator can do (implicitly, without needing explicit curator assignments), plus infrastructure management:

- **Reshape a world view without erasing anyone's travels.** Deleting, flattening or merging away a region that a traveller has marked as visited is refused, in the World View Editor and in the import review alike, and the screen says so — the editor with the number of visits standing in the way. A refused edit is not half done: the region's children and members stay where they were. Flattening all of a region's subregions at once stops at the first one that is refused, and says how many were flattened before it. Deleting a whole world view is the one act that removes visits, and its confirmation counts them first
- **Decide which sources need a person's eye** — one switch per source, reading "Hold new and changed content for review", with the consequence written under it rather than left to be discovered. Held means new objects from that source arrive invisible to visitors and a change to an object they already see is kept back, until a curator passes it. What the switch never does is take something away: everything published before it was flipped stays exactly as visible as it was, so one source can hold newly-held objects beside long-published ones. Turning it off is equally quiet — the click itself publishes nothing, because a switch that made forty unreviewed objects visible in one go is exactly what holding them back exists to prevent. What becomes of a backlog afterward is two answers rather than one, and the panel gives both beside the switch in either of its positions — while the gate is on as what the click would do, once it is off as where things now stand, and as kinds without numbers where the counting failed. Objects nobody has read, and unread points or works, keep waiting until a person releases them, while a change that was being held back is applied by that source's next run — holding it back was the gate's doing, and the gate is gone. The panel also says, per source, what is waiting and in what shape: objects nobody has read, objects visitors can see that are holding a change, objects visitors can see that are holding unread points or works. Three sentences rather than one number, because "eighteen waiting" means something different for eighteen unread museums than for eighteen paintings inside one published museum. And where the counting itself fails, the panel says *that* — "how much this source is holding could not be counted" — and offers no release for that source until it can name what the release would publish. "Nothing waiting" is the sentence an admin acts on by leaving a held source alone, so it is not a sentence to print when nobody counted; the sources, and everything needed to run or stop a sync, are still there either way. What the switch still says in that state is both answers **as kinds rather than counts** — unread things would wait for a person, a change being held back would be applied by the next run — because how much is unknown while what-becomes-of-it is not, and that second half is the only consequence here a flip back cannot undo: once a run has applied a held change, re-holding the source restores nothing. Places of worship is the first source that arrives with the switch already on: a source anybody in the world can edit is one whose first rows are worth a person's eye, so a **Dry run** answers what it would write, and the run that follows fills a curator's queue rather than the map
- **Set how famous a place has to be** — the places-of-worship source states its own line on its card in the sync panel: *Enter at* and *Stay at or above*, two counts of Wikipedia languages, with a sentence saying what they mean and that the next run is what applies them. The count is exactly that: how many language editions of Wikipedia have an article, and nothing else that links a place — a Commons category or a Wikivoyage page does not raise it. Twenty-two to enter and eighteen to stay is where it starts, and the two numbers are two rather than one so a list does not flap as Wikipedia grows — a cathedral that slips a language does not drop out and come back next month. Nothing already synced moves when the line is saved, and the save does not start a run. The two Wikidata sources before it keep their line in code and show no fields at all, rather than offering a change no run would read, and the World Heritage list has no line to state — it is the source's own membership; a screen for comparing what several lines would admit is still to come
- **One Start Sync brings a source up to date** — the admin's Sync card offers one button per source, *Start Sync*, and it does every step a current catalogue needs, in order and reported as steps of the run: reads the source, finds the Wikidata items of the World Heritage components the run has just left without one, and pairs what the run changed with near places of similar names. A step that fails, or cannot run — the search on a machine where the project's own copy of Wikidata is not built yet — is named on the run's closing line with its reason, and the steps after it still run. What used to be four more buttons reads as what it is: the run's modes — a preview, a run without the cache, a run that asks again about pins — are options behind the button's arrow, and the single-purpose actions sit under *More actions*, each with a sentence saying when it is needed: *Fix pictures* for now, since it writes past the review gate, and *Find component items* over every site or one for after a rule of the search changes, about eight minutes over the whole list.
- **Follow a long operation step by step** — *Find component items*, *Fix pictures*, placing a world view's places into its regions, computing a world view's geometry, extracting from Wikivoyage and importing or re-matching a world view say which step they are on and of how many (*Step 2 of 12 · Reading the names of 9922 candidate items*), and building a copy from a public dump which step it is on, since it names its steps as it goes; each shows a bar that measures that step, how much of it is done, how long the step and the whole have run, and when Wikidata or Commons is holding the run, how long the wait has left. Before, the search showed one sentence over a bar that measured nothing for five to ten minutes. The backend log carries the same steps, one line as each starts and ends
- **Run syncs** — trigger and monitor data synchronization from external sources (UNESCO, Wikidata for museums, landmarks, places of worship). A **Dry run** answers "what would this change?" without changing anything: it fetches from the source, works out the difference against what is stored, and records the result as a preview that writes no experiences. The same run can be previewed as often as needed before it is spent. **A sync cannot destroy anything a person recorded.** There is one kind of run, and it refreshes what the source still offers, adds what is new, and marks what the source stopped offering — visits, the individual points people ticked off, the artworks they logged as seen, curator edits and manual region assignments all survive it. The old **Force sync**, which emptied the source's whole catalogue and every visit record with it before starting again, is gone: with nothing left to delete it was a mode that differed from an ordinary run in nothing but the damage. A museum run also has to have seen enough of the works its museums hold before it may say a work has left one: a run that placed fewer than nine in ten of them again marks nothing, is reported as partial rather than successful, and its run card says how many of how many it placed — so a short answer from the source reads as a short answer, never as a museum losing its collection
- **Run kinds drawn from the same candidates together** — the kinds of place read from Wikidata pick from one pool of candidates: Public art, Monuments & memorials, Places of worship, Archaeology, Towers, Bridges & engineering marvels and Squares & streets are filled from one reading of Wikidata, in one run, and the Motherland Calls is offered by both monument kinds. Each kind still decides and writes its own places, with its own count, admissions and report, and sees only the candidates its own classes name, so Public art never reports a cathedral it refused. On the sync panel they share one card: the buttons that start and stop the run appear once, with a line saying whose Wikidata switch the shared reading follows. Below them each kind has a section of its own with how its run went, whether its arrivals wait for a curator, what is waiting, its own Wikidata switch and cache, which the rest of its run reads through, and its own repairs, such as *Fix pictures*. When the run starts, each section follows its own kind, so a later kind says it is waiting for the earlier ones and then shows its own progress. Starting the card starts them all, and a Cancel stops them all. The card refuses to start while the kinds' Wikidata switches disagree, or while one of the kinds is switched off, and says which: one run must not read two dates of Wikidata. Places of worship and Archaeology can no longer be run on their own. The run history keeps their runs together, and a later kind's run says which run's reading it used
- **See which public dumps the catalogue can read, and that they arrive whole** — under *For developers* on the *Public Dumps* page, the section lists the bulk files the catalogue is built from in one table per publisher (Wikidata, the English Wikipedia's tables, the redirects of every Wikipedia edition the readership counts, the monthly readership counts): each with what it holds and how its last build ended — verified or failed, when, the date of the dump, its size and lines, and what it was checked against — with the builds before it a click away. *Verify newest* finds the newest file its host has finished publishing — never one still being written — reads it through end to end, and checks it against the checksum the host published (or, for the readership counts, which have none, the host's size and a clean end of the file); the build says how many bytes and lines it read and whether the file was whole. A build can be cancelled, and one that fails says why. A build runs in a worker of its own, so restarting or updating the server does not stop it: the row says it is waiting for the worker, then shows its progress, about how long the pass it is reading has left, and when it was last seen alive, whatever runs it, and no second build of that dump can start until it ends, so two downloads of the same 156 GB file never fill the disk. Reading one of these files once replaces thousands of questions to a live service, which is how the catalogue stays a good guest of the sources it reads. The redirects' row builds, from each counted edition's own tables, which titles lead readers to which article, so a painting read mostly under its old title is counted as read: *Wanderer Above the Sea of Fog* gets the 388,094 views a year its one-letter redirect carries, and the readership year is read again with them
- **Keep the catalogue's copy of Wikidata current** — *Public Dumps* opens on one card for the Wikidata copy: what it is, which dump it was built from, and a notice only when something needs doing: a newer dump is out (*Update*, several hours, the catalogue keeps working), or an update of the app needs the copy's tables recomputed (*Recompute*, minutes) or the copy rebuilt (*Update*). Otherwise it reads "Up to date", once the dumps site has answered: until then it says it is checking, and when the site does not answer it says a newer dump may be out. The tools for changing how the copy is made sit apart, in a collapsed *For developers* section.
- **Build the local copy of Wikidata the catalogue reads** — under *For developers*, the *All entities* row of *Public Dumps* has a *Build newest* button beside *Verify newest*, and the row says which dump the copy in place was built from and when (or that it was never built). Where a dump file is already on the server's disk, the row says its date and size, and *Build \<date\> copy* builds from it without downloading the newest dump, still checked against the published checksum; a newer dump replaces it, so the disk holds one copy of each dump: the older copy is removed once the newer one is verified, or before the download when the disk cannot hold both, and *Verify newest* and *Build newest* say so on hover while a local copy is there. The monthly readership counts are twelve different months, not copies of one another, and each is removed from the disk once its month is kept. Every Wikidata source whose questions the local copy can all answer has a switch on its card in the sync panel between the live Wikidata service and that local copy: on the local copy its runs no longer depend on the live service answering, and read Wikidata as of the copy's dump date. A build reads the dump twice: once to find what the catalogue keeps (every place with a coordinate, every class, the works and objects that name a place, the rooms and wings between an object and its museum), and once to keep it. Its progress says which step it is on, how far that step has got where it can tell (the labels it reads back, the parts of a derivation, the index being built), and when the step started, and the row says plainly when the worker has gone quiet and may have stopped. The new copy replaces the old one only when it is whole, so a build that fails or is cancelled leaves the catalogue reading what it read before. Once a copy is in place, *Derive again* rebuilds its statements, names, Wikipedia links and class tree from the items it already holds, without reading the dump: when the way those are made changes, the copy catches up in the time that step takes rather than the hours two passes over the dump take, and the row says when it was last derived again, keeping the build that read the file as its last build, since a re-derive reads none. To try a change to how the copy is built without waiting hours, *Cut a sample* copies the first few gigabytes of the dump on disk into a small file of its own, and *Try the build* runs every step of a build on it, says what it kept and how long each step took, and then throws the result away: the copy the catalogue reads is never touched
- **Keep a year of Wikipedia readership** — under *For developers*, the *Readers per article, by month* row of *Public Dumps* has *Build newest* too: it keeps how often each place's and work's Wikipedia articles were read, per language, over the last twelve complete months, the signal the fame doors of the kinds will read beside sitelinks. A first build downloads and reads every month and takes some hours; a later build reads only the months it lacks and drops the month that left the year. Each month is checked whole before it is kept, a month that fails leaves the earlier ones kept for the next build to continue from, and each month's file is removed from the disk once it is kept. The row says which month the year ends at and whether all twelve are there; there is no sample or trial, no *Derive again* and no build from a copy on disk, since a month is kept only from its whole file and the year always ends at the newest month.
- **Keep English Wikipedia's categories** — under *For developers*, the *Pages* row of the English Wikipedia's tables on *Public Dumps* has *Build newest*: it keeps which category each English Wikipedia article and category is filed under, with each article's Wikidata item, from the four tables of one dump date, each checked whole before the next is read, so the kinds can read Wikipedia's own grouping — *Archaeological museums by country* and the categories under it — without asking Wikipedia's servers hundreds of questions a run. The maintenance and tracking categories readers never see are left out. The new tables replace the old only when all four are kept, so a build that fails or is cancelled leaves the catalogue reading what it read before; there is no sample or trial, since a table is kept only from its whole file.
- **Keep English Wikivoyage's articles** — under *For developers*, the *English Wikivoyage's articles* row of the Wikivoyage editions on *Public Dumps* has *Build newest*: it keeps every article of English Wikivoyage with its text, every redirect with the page it leads to, and each article's Wikidata item, from one monthly dump checked whole against its published checksum, which the Wikivoyage extraction of a world view reads instead of the live wiki, whose answers changed between two runs a few days apart. The row says which dump the copy was built from; the new copy replaces the old one only when it is whole, and there is no sample or trial, since the articles are kept only from the whole file.
- **Keep the OpenStreetMap layer** — under *For developers*, the OpenStreetMap *Planet* row of *Public Dumps* has *Build newest* and *Build \<date\> copy*: the first time it downloads the weekly planet file and checks it whole against its published md5; every build then brings the planet up to last midnight with OpenStreetMap's daily change files, about 100 MB a day, and cuts from it the objects the catalogue asks about — anything tagged with a Wikidata item or a Wikipedia article, the historic, heritage, archaeological, tourism, man-made, place and protected-area objects, and every object a place in the catalogue already links, whatever its tags — with every tag. It keeps the full outline of the ruins, digs and protected areas a site is drawn from, and one point for every other object; an outline it does not keep is asked of OpenStreetMap when a run needs it. The new layer replaces the old one only when it is whole, so a build that fails or is cancelled leaves the catalogue reading what it read before. The row says which day the layer is current to; a build that only brought the planet forward is shown as *Built*, not *Verified*, since it downloaded no weekly file. *Build \<date\> copy* starts the planet again from a weekly file already on disk, which is also the way out when the planet kept cannot be brought forward. Once the layer has been built, the worker brings it up to date by itself, every 7 days unless the admin picks another interval, from every day to every 30 days, or turns it off, under the same row. A self-started update shows as queued half an hour before it begins, saying when, so it can be cancelled.
- **Release everything one source is holding, in one act** — for a source being turned off, or one whose backlog was never the point, there is a single "Publish all waiting" beside it rather than a queue to walk. It says what it will do before it does it, and says it per kind rather than as one number: how many objects nobody has read become visible to visitors, and — separately — how many objects visitors can already see gain the points and works waiting under them without themselves “becoming visible”, because they are visible already. Each is recorded on its own, so the history still says when a museum became visible, and what was released under one visitors already had. It also says what it will **not** do — a change to an object visitors can already see is never published in a batch, because publishing it rewrites what they are looking at with a value nobody read, and that belongs on the card that shows the old wording beside the new one. So those stay waiting on purpose, the notice says how many — or says outright that it could not count them, where the count itself failed after the publications had landed, because a zero there would be a claim about the source nobody checked. **Nothing a single publication would have told them is dropped because there are forty of them**: how many points and works became visible, how many replaced pins stopped being shown and in which objects, and which objects landed with their regions not recomputed, named with the world views an admin has to rebuild. Read off a batch of forty, each of them is otherwise recoverable only by opening forty histories, which is the work this button exists to avoid. A curator whose scope is a set of regions releases what they cover and is told how many were outside it, instead of reading a short result as a cleared source
- **The places that are one place are one** — the catalogue holds a place once per kind it was brought in by, so the Pantheon was two entries — a place of worship and an archaeological site — two pins on one spot and two cards that each told half of it, and the National Museum of Archaeology in Madrid was three. Where two sources name the same Wikidata item the answer is not in doubt, and nobody presses a button for it: a source that reads a Wikidata item, when that item names exactly one place and the place does not have the source's kind yet, finds that place as it arrives and adds its kind to it, instead of making a second entry. A curator sees the arrival as a new kind on a place visitors already see, and the place keeps one pin even where the two sources put it hundreds of metres apart, the Cave of Altamira among them; which coordinate stands is the curator's question. The one way two entries can still end up claiming one item is an item learnt late — a heritage site's run, or a curator confirming which Wikidata item one place of a serial site is, naming an item a tower or a temple already is — and the catalogue makes the two one as that run or that answer ends, offered in both kinds, keeping the entry visitors find in a list, the older one where both are listed; where neither is listed yet — one place of a heritage site, seen only under its site, beside a tower nobody has passed: the Tower of Belém — the site's place keeps its entry and the arrival joins it, still waiting for a curator. Travellers' visits move with it, the earlier date and both notes where someone marked both. Every merge can be undone from the place's history. A World Heritage site counts too, when its inscription is one place: Chartres Cathedral, a heritage site and a cathedral, becomes one place with one pin, the cathedral's point and the site's made one. A site inscribed as many places — the churches of Chiloé, the Roman frontier's forts — is not merged as a whole with any one of them, and a site whose inscription names two things, Venice beside its lagoon, is not merged on a guess. And Catalogue Checks lists every Wikidata item or World Heritage reference two places each still claim to be, from the catalogue's register of what identifies each place: a pair the catalogue could not make one — two entries in the same kind — or a reference only a curator can settle, so it does not go unnoticed.
- **Separate a kind that is another place** — when the shared Wikidata item turned out to name something else, a curator takes that kind out of the place, on the place's dialog: *Separate Archaeology*, then a confirmation that says what happens. The kind becomes a place of its own, with the points and works it places, and keeps whether it was published. A point or a work both kinds place is given to each. The original keeps its other kinds and its visits; the shared identifier is now the new place's, written as the curator's word, so no run joins the two again and the near-places pass never proposes them. Separated by mistake, the two are put back together from the original's history (*Undo this separation*), and that merge can itself be undone. A traveller who marked the original visited is asked which of the two places they visited (#1887), and until they answer the visit stays where they put it. It is the way back from a merge, and from a source finding a place by an identifier it carries (ADR-0144).
- **Find the places that may be one place** — a shared Wikidata item settles the pairs it reaches; the rest show themselves by standing together under alike names. Nobody has to ask: a place is looked at again whenever its name, its points, its kinds or whether readers see it change — a run, a publish, a curator's edit, a merge or a separation — and the whole catalogue is looked at again once whenever the rule itself changes, so a pair reaches the review queue on its own. The rule finds every pair of places within reach of each other — 300 m, or a World Heritage property's own extent up to 3 km — whose names are alike enough: the Statue of Liberty as a World Heritage site and as public art, Durham Cathedral beside the site "Durham Castle and Cathedral", the National Gallery of Ireland 160 m from the National Museum of Ireland. A short name inside a long World Heritage title counts too, where every word of the one stands in the other — Geghard beside "Monastery of Geghard and the Upper Azat Valley", Westminster Abbey beside "Palace of Westminster and Westminster Abbey" — and its card says that rather than how little the two names look alike. Each is a question for a person — one place, two places, or one part of the other — never a merge on a guess, and a pair answered once is not asked about again unless a curator takes the answer back. Two places in one kind are left apart, since one place cannot be in a kind twice; the check *Two near places with similar names nobody has answered* in Catalogue Checks counts the pairs still waiting. A part of a serial site is weighed like any place — Svetitskhoveli Cathedral, one of the Historical Monuments of Mtskheta, beside the place of worship of the same name — while the serial site as a whole is not weighed against the places inside it, and a place nobody has published yet is not weighed at all.
- **Say what two near places are** — each pair the pass found comes to the review queue as a question on the older entry's card, with the other place beside it: its picture, the kinds it is in — or, for a part of a serial site, the site it is part of — its Wikidata entry next to this one's — where the two have different entries, each beside its own place with what Wikidata says it is, so Babylon the ancient city is told from Babylon the World Heritage listing, and a place with no entry is said to have none — how far apart the two stand and how alike the names are. The curator has three answers. *One place* folds the other entry into this one — one pin, both kinds, every traveller's visits kept — and the history can undo it like any merge. *Two places* keeps them apart, and the pair is never asked about again. *Part of* says which is the part — Durham Cathedral within the site "Durham Castle and Cathedral" — and merges nothing. A pair that carries one and the same name within fifty metres is marked, and one button answers every such pair *one place* at once; a pair merely alike is read on its own. Answering a selection from the list makes one place only of those marked pairs and keeps every other pair two, so nothing is merged on a guess. Where the deployment uses Jev, each pair also says what Jev makes of it — *one place*, *two places* or *one part of the other* — and how sure it is, as a suggestion beside the facts, never chosen for the curator; one button answers *one place* for every pair Jev calls one place and is sure of, while which is the part stays the curator's, and an admin sees in AI Settings what those judgements cost and how often curators agreed, beside the same figures for the other cards.
- **Leave Wikidata's parts to Wikidata, and take any answer back** — many near pairs are a whole and something inside it, and Wikidata often says so already: the Sassi di Matera's entry says it is part of Matera's World Heritage site, Westminster Abbey's that it is part of "Palace of Westminster and Westminster Abbey including Saint Margaret's Church". Such a pair is answered *part of* by the catalogue itself, and a pair proposed with that statement already in place never reaches the queue; the place's history names the catalogue as the one who answered and shows the statement it read. Only an entry's own *part of* or *has part* counts. That the Florence Baptistery stands in the Historic Centre of Florence leaves the pair for a curator, and so do two entries that each claim to be the other's part. On the development catalogue that is half the pairs waiting, 13 of 26. A curator who disagrees, with the catalogue or with another curator's *part of* or *two places*, presses *Take back this answer* on that row of the history: the pair is a question on the queue again, and the catalogue does not answer it a second time. *One place* is taken back the way it always was, by undoing the merge.
- **See which parts of a serial site Wikidata knows** — a World Heritage run now ties each place a serial site is made of to its own Wikidata entry where there is exactly one: 137 of the 137 stretches of the Qhapaq Ñan, 108 of the 111 pile-dwelling villages around the Alps, none of Roman Dacia's 277 forts. The run's details say how many resolved, which references two entries share — left unresolved rather than guessed — and which sites are left short, the first step towards each part having its own picture and card.
- **See what a sync actually did** — a run opens into a per-object report rather than four totals: which objects were created, which fields changed on which object (and whether the change matters — a site entering the danger list is not a reworded description), which came through untouched, which the source stopped listing, where the source now disagrees with a curator's edit, and which entries the source offered that its kind does not hold — a catalogue's collection is not a museum, and setting it aside is not a failure. **An object whose *contents* moved is one of those changes, and reads as its own kind.** A run that added a place to a serial site, or hung a work in a museum, used to leave nothing in the report at all: every field of the object had come through, so there was no row. Now there is one, marked **contents** rather than updated — not counted among the run's updates, because the object did not change, the museum did — and it names what arrived rather than counting it: "places: gained Waldsiedlung Zehlendorf". The report defaults to the changes worth a look, and what it hides is a minor field edit that moved nothing else: a row whose contents moved stays even when the only edited field was a reworded description, because that row is the only record anywhere that the component arrived, and a row where the source ran into a curator's own edit stays too, however small the field, because a run's one disagreement with a person is the row an admin opens the report to find. A change a gated source proposed and the gate kept back reads as exactly that, and never as a change that landed: the object is marked as **held** rather than updated, it is not counted among the run's updates, and the field says which value visitors are still seeing and which one is waiting for someone to look. That is a different mark from a field where the run ran into a curator's own edit, because the two say opposite things about who is waiting — one has already been decided, the other has not been looked at at all. **A number the import keeps about its own run is not a change.** How many works a pass hung in a museum, and how famous those works add up to being, are the catalogue's own bookkeeping — and the second moves whenever anybody anywhere adds a language link to any painting a museum holds. Behind a gate, every one of those moves became a question: a card asked a person to approve the Louvre's fame total going from 2363 to 2365, and the next run asked again, for nearly every museum, with no end to it. Worse, the answer never arrived, so the number a person was being asked about was also the number that never got written: the stored total stayed at what it read the day the gate went up, while every run since worked out a different one. Nobody saw a wrong figure — that number is shown on no screen today, and the order museums come out in is worked out fresh each run rather than read back from it. What there was, was a question nobody could answer and a record that had quietly stopped following the thing it records. Those numbers now go straight into the row, and appear in no report and on no card. What was counted is still reported exactly as before: a painting arriving in a museum is a thing that happened to the museum, and that is the part somebody can actually answer for. **And the run's own summary says how many of its changes were held back.** A run from a gated source used to report itself as one that touched nothing — "created 0, updated 0, unchanged 1272" — while each of those 1272 was a change to a visible object that a curator had yet to answer, visible only from the curator's queue, which the admin reading the run list may not be the person watching. The run list and the run's card now carry that number beside the others, and it climbs while a run is still going, so "the source said nothing new" and "the source proposed a change a person must answer" stop reading the same. It is the count of held *changes*, and only that: a whole new object is already in the Created figure, and a new point or work arriving inside an object visitors can see is neither — those two are counted per source rather than per run, on the gate's own panel, which says all three kinds side by side. **A run that declined a step says which and why**, each on a line of its own on its card: missing detection (a ranked source, or too little seen to call anything gone), withdrawals (too few of a museum's works seen to say which left) and the admission sweep (a run that finished with errors, admitted nothing or admitted under half what it did before leaves the places its kind's rule would delist where they were). A run whose withdrawals or sweep were declined reads as **partial** rather than a success, since the catalogue was left as it was and a person should look at why.
- **Region assignment is not a step to remember** — a sync places whatever moved during the run, so an admin no longer has to notice that new objects arrived and press anything. **What a run remembers, and how to make it forget.** A source's runs keep what the outside world told them — the class trees, the pools of famous works, where each work says it hangs — so that a collection interrupted half-way resumes instead of starting over, and so a question whose answer has not changed is not asked again. None of it is ever shown to a visitor: it only decides whether a sync asks again. Each source's card carries its own cache, closed until an admin opens it, listing what is in it, how old the oldest part is, when the next part expires, and how long each part lives by rule — with the reason for that rule in the same sentence, because "an ontology moves over months" is what makes a week a defensible number. The lifetime can be changed per part, and the change applies to what is already there: an answer keeps its own age, so shortening the rule can expire it at once, and the panel says how many were re-dated. Any part can be cleared, which means "ask again". And a single run can ignore the whole cache without deleting it. A source switched to read Wikidata from the local subset keeps none of Wikidata's answers: its card names the dump the subset was built from, still shows what the source asks a live service (the archaeology run's OpenStreetMap answers), and lists apart what its earlier runs on the live service left, which no run reads: the source's next run deletes what is a week past its expiry, and Clear deletes it at once. Only the sources that actually keep something show any of this — a card that offered to clear a cache the source never fills would be inventing an explanation.

- **A work names everyone who made it.** Plenty of famous things were made by more than one person, and the catalogue used to name one of them — picked, in effect, at random, because the source lists them in no particular order and the importer kept whichever arrived first. So *Morning in a Pine Forest* was credited to Konstantin Savitsky, who painted the bears, and not to Ivan Shishkin, who painted the forest; *The Feast of the Gods* to Titian, who reworked the landscape, and not to Giovanni Bellini, who painted it; the *Laocoön* to one of the three sculptors of Rhodes. Now every name the source gives is stored and shown. **What the catalogue does not claim is which of them led** — because the source does not say. It records who made a thing and not in what order, and the order our own import happens to receive them in is the database's, not Wikidata's. So two names read as what they are, "Ivan Shishkin and Konstantin Savitsky", and where a row has one line to spend and there are more than two, it says "6 artists" rather than picking one of the six to put in front. That is a curator's call, and once a curator has made it the row leads with the name they put first — and no import can shuffle it afterwards. A curator is also no longer asked to choose between "Shishkin and Savitsky" and "Savitsky and Shishkin": the source restating the same people in another order is not a change, and nothing is asked about it

- **Every picture is one the catalogue is allowed to show.** The World Heritage Centre's own photographs — four pictures in five, until now — come with terms that forbid copying them or drawing them inside another site, so they are not drawn here any more. A World Heritage site's picture now comes from Wikimedia Commons, whose photographs are published to be reused with the photographer named, found through the site's own entry in Wikidata rather than by guessing from its name: the Sydney Opera House, Cologne Cathedral and the Acropolis all keep a picture, and so do 96 in every 100. The few dozen that Commons has no photograph of show none, honestly, and keep the link to the site's own page at UNESCO, which is what its terms invite. An administrator repairs the stored pictures from the sync panel with one button — *Fix pictures* — rather than waiting for a curator to approve twelve hundred cards. And nothing sits between a reader and a picture any more: Commons sizes its own files, where before a free third-party service resized most of them, with no agreement behind it and no say in when it changed its mind.
- **A part with no picture of its own can be given one from Commons.** Where no Wikidata entry gives a part of a serial site its picture, the curator correcting that part is offered the photographs Wikimedia Commons has geotagged within 300 m of it — at Paimio Sanatorium, twelve files, the nearest taken 8 m away — each with how far off it was taken and whose photograph it is. A geotag says where the camera stood, not what it shows, so the curator looks and picks the one that shows the place; nothing is written until they save it, and the photograph is then credited wherever the part's picture appears.
- **A part of a serial site can be told which Wikidata entry it is.** Most of a serial World Heritage site's parts find their Wikidata entry by their UNESCO number, and that entry gives each part's card its own picture and description. About two in five carry no number anywhere — the forts of the Roman frontier in Dacia, most of the D-Day beaches, several of Alvar Aalto's buildings. An administrator now presses *Find component items* on the World Heritage source to look for them — for every site, or for one, by its id beside the button: after correcting a point of the Dacian frontier, the site's own card offers *Search this site again* while it has candidates to answer, which asks about that site's parts and nothing else. The search reads the project's own copy of Wikidata, built from its public dump, so a busy public service no longer stops it halfway, and its closing line says which dump the copy was built from. An entry qualifies in one of several ways. It may say it is part of the site, like Villa Mairea within Aalto's works, so long as it stands near the part and is named like it. It may be a place of the same sort as the site's other parts, standing within a few hundred metres and named like it, like a Roman fort beside the frontier's other forts. Or it may carry the part's UNESCO number as an earlier or later inscription of the site numbers it, like the kofun of Mozu-Furuichi, so long as it is named like the part — an extension can renumber the parts, so the number alone never decides. Or, near the part and of the same sort, its name may carry a place from the part's own name — the Roman forts of Dacia are listed as "Turda - Potaissa" and named on Wikidata "Castrul roman Potaissa", which a plain comparison of names misses. Where an entry carries a national heritage register's code, the card shows it: an entry the register files as architecture, offered for a Roman fort among archaeology entries, is said to be another kind of entry and listed below them. A village that gave a fort its name is never taken for the fort. Nothing is written onto the part by the search but the candidates whose answer is already known (below): each other candidate waits for a curator on the review page, under *Components with candidate Wikidata items* — one card per site, the candidates listed under their parts with why each was found, how far off it stands, how alike the names are and a link to the entry. The site reads as what it is made of — the card says "made of 7 places" rather than one coordinate, and each part is a small card of its own with its own picture — and one map on the card shows every place of the site, named, with the candidate the curator picks beside its part and the distance drawn between them. A candidate is picked by clicking its row, or with the row's *Show on the map* button from the keyboard, so the map stays on the pair being judged while the pointer travels over the list to it; until a pick, the map's place says to pick one. The map stands on the pair in its middle, close enough that a candidate about ten metres or more from its part shows as a pin of its own, and draws the site's other places around them for a curator to zoom out to: whether Ochtinská Aragonite Cave, 11 m from the Koniar plateau's point, is that part is read off the map at a look. The curator says of each whether it is the same place; candidates that carry the part's own name at its own spot are marked, and one button confirms them all, except where two compete for one part or one entry, which the curator picks between. A confirmed entry becomes the part's, its picture and description follow where the entry has them, and the choice is kept through every later run; one turned down is never offered again. A confirmation can be taken back from the site's curation history — the row that recorded it offers *Take back* for each entry it confirmed — and the part is then as it was: no entry, no picture or description the entry gave it, and the candidate is back on the card, marked *taken back*, to be turned down or confirmed again by hand — never by either one-button confirmation, since a hasty *Confirm all* is taken back as often as a wrong entry. A batch answer from the list confirms only the marked candidates, since the rest need reading. **A part the list places only roughly is asked about too**: where the World Heritage list gives a part only to the whole minute — a cell about two kilometres across, which puts the Longmen Grottoes 10 km from their point — or gives several parts one shared point, as it does Echmiatsin's cathedral, church and cemetery, the site gets a card *Where is it?* with each such part, why its point is rough, and what says where it really stands: the OpenStreetMap object that carries the part's own World Heritage number (Saint Gayane church, 341 m off), the part's own Wikidata entry, a candidate entry, a nearby object of its name, each with its distance and a link. The map shows the point, the hint picked, the minute it was given to and the site's other places. The curator moves the part to a hint, places it by hand, or keeps the list's point where it stands for a large area well enough, and the part is not asked about again. Only a part something says more about is asked: a village the part is named after is never offered as where it stands, and a part nothing says more about is listed apart, to be kept or placed by hand, until a later search finds it a hint. A site that one point stands for and that is many times larger than the uncertainty, the Okavango Delta or Virunga, is not asked at all; a compact one like the Longmen Grottoes still is. Catalogue Checks counts both lists. Where the deployment uses Jev, each hint also says what Jev makes of it — *the same place* or *another place* — and how sure it is, as a suggestion beside the facts; a hint Jev is sure is another place, *Abrigo de las Figuras Diversas* offered for *Abrigo de las figuras Amarillas*, is listed after the rest, and AI Settings shows what those judgements cost and how often curators agreed. Until a curator has placed such a part, a visitor is told: its pin on the map is a ring rather than a dot, and its card and its row say the location is approximate and why, so nobody walks to the Longmen Grottoes' point and finds a field 10 km from the caves. **A candidate whose answer is already known is not asked at all**: one carrying the part's own name within 50 m of it, the only such candidate of its part, for an entry offered for no other part of the site and with no heritage register against it, is recorded by *Find component items* itself — the Nagatsuka Kofun's entry, 7 m from its point. Of the 151 such candidates on 2026-10-22, curators had confirmed every one they answered, and every wrong candidate was a name only partly alike. The site's history shows the confirmation as the catalogue's own — *The catalogue — the same name at the point* — and offers *Take back* for it like a curator's; one taken back is never confirmed again but by a curator. Where the deployment uses Jev, each candidate also says what Jev makes of it — *the same place* or *another place* — and how sure it is, as a suggestion beside the facts, never chosen for the curator; one button confirms every candidate Jev calls the same place and is sure of — except one that competes for a part or an entry with any candidate either button would take, which the curator picks between, so the two buttons never undo each other — and an admin sees in AI Settings what those judgements cost and how often curators agreed, beside the same figures for the sources card.
- **The photographer is named.** Almost every picture in the catalogue was taken by somebody and is served from Wikimedia Commons, and most carry a licence that asks one thing of anyone showing it: that the person who took it is credited wherever it appears. For a long time the catalogue credited nobody. Now a quiet line under each picture says who took it and under what terms, linked to where those terms are stated: *Stefan Kühn · CC BY-SA 3.0* under Stonehenge, *Benoît Prieur · CC0* under the Little Mermaid. It is deliberately small — it is owed to the photographer, not offered to the reader — and where a source names nobody, nothing is shown rather than an empty line implying nobody took it. **A credit never outlives its picture either**: where the photograph does not arrive, the line goes with it, rather than naming a person beside an empty frame. **A curator who puts a different picture on an object takes the old credit off with it**: the line either names whoever took the new one, or says nothing. What it never does is leave one person's name under another person's photograph. **The works inside a museum are credited on the same terms as the museum itself** — the photographs of paintings, statues and steles that the catalogue shows and does not own. Most of them are of paintings old enough to be free of copyright, and there the file names the painter, whom the row already names; a line repeating the artist and adding "Public domain" is not a credit, it is noise that teaches a reader to stop looking at the place a real credit will appear. So on a list the line draws where it carries something: a licence that asks to be honoured — the Mesha Stele in the Louvre was photographed by Mbzt under CC BY-SA 4.0 — or a photographer who is not the artist. Rest on a work and the picture fills the map at a size worth looking at, with the whole credit under it. **The screens curators work on name the photographer too** — the review queue's cards and the picture they enlarge, the preview of what is waiting to be published, the search results when an object is being added. Working on the catalogue rather than publishing it does not change whose photograph it is. And a curator who creates an object by hand has its picture credited from the moment they save it, rather than waiting for an import that will never look at a row a person wrote.

- **What you have seen can be ticked off without a mouse.** The two lists a visitor records against — the places a serial site is made of, and the works a museum holds — are where you say what you have actually stood in front of, and until now both took a pointer: someone reading with a keyboard could go through every work in the Louvre, or every one of a heritage site's parts, and record none of them. Neither list shows itself whole, so the way in mattered as much as the rows, and the two lists hide themselves differently. On the object's own page both are folded shut past fifteen entries: the heading that opens each is now a control the keyboard reaches, and it says whether what you are about to open is already open. Beside the map the works list has no fold at all — it shows ten and keeps the rest behind a single "show all", which was likewise a thing only a mouse could press, so a reader could tick off ten of the Louvre's holdings and reach none of the other hundred. Inside, each row is a stop the keyboard reaches, and what answers depends on what the control is: the tick box beside a place or a work takes Space, while the tiles and the headings, being buttons, take Space or Enter. What a screen reader announces is the thing itself and what the press will *do* with it: "Ħaġar Qim — mark as visited", "The Night Watch — mark as seen", and the opposite once it is. Before, the tick boxes had no names at all, so a reader heard "checkbox, not checked" and had to guess which of thirty places it belonged to. The same holds for every control drawn as an icon: closing a place's panel, opening its Wikipedia article, its website or its UNESCO page, opening the account menu — each is announced by what it does, not as "button". Where the keyboard is is visible, too — whatever it has reached is outlined, which on a grid of near-identical squares is the difference between navigating and guessing, and which every control in the product gained at once rather than these two alone. **And a visitor who is not signed in is no longer offered the control at all** — they keep the picture, its title and its credit, where before they met a tile that invited a click and answered it with silence, because recording anything needs an account to record it against.

**Being a good guest of the sources we import from.** Every source is somebody else's server, and the rules are theirs. The World Heritage list now arrives in a single request rather than thirteen paged ones — the portal allows ten thousand calls a day and publishes what is left of them, which the run reads and records — and asking it for only the fields the catalogue actually uses cut the answer from 24 MB to under 4. That last change also ended a long silence. The importer had been asking for an inscription-criteria field that does not exist in that dataset, so **not one** of the 1,272 heritage sites carried its criteria; previewing the fix turned up two more of the same kind, and the catalogue was recording **none** of the 58 sites the world lists as in danger and **none** of the 51 that cross a border. All three are read correctly now, and where an admin has put that source behind the curation gate, the missing years arrive as one batch of proposals to approve rather than as a silent rewrite. The 58 sites in danger were the exception and were simply put right: a curator's job is to judge what a source says, and being asked 58 times to approve the correction of our own misreading is not that job — nobody would have seen the mark until they had finished, either. All of it — the heritage portal and the three Wikidata imports alike — now waits out a bad day at the source instead of ending the run on the first refusal, and shares one quarter-hour of patience across a phase of a run rather than granting it per question — the collecting half and the crediting half each get one, so neither can spend the other's.

**A run that is waiting says it is waiting.** Before the first object is written there is nothing to count — nobody knows how many museums the source will offer until it answers — so the panel shows a moving indeterminate bar and the phase it is in, rather than a percentage parked at zero, which reads as a run that has hung. And when the source itself is having a bad day, the panel says so in those words: which error it gave, that we are retrying, how long until the next attempt, and how much of the waiting is spent. That is the difference between an admin watching a stuck screen and an admin deciding to come back in ten minutes — a real museum run failed exactly there, after a minute of invisible retries. The run says the rest while it happens too: after the last object it reports **assigning regions**, and for how many objects, rather than finishing, for as long as that takes — seconds, even when a source's first run has brought in a thousand places — with the name of the last object gone from under the bar, since nothing is handling it any more, and Cancel is unavailable from the moment the last object is processed — through the tidying up and the assigning that follow — because by then there is nothing left for a stop to interrupt, and pretending otherwise would report a cancellation that never happened. **Cancel is answered while the run is waiting, too.** A collection that is riding out a bad quarter-hour at the source can be minutes from its next question, and a stop that only took effect when the waiting ended was, from the admin's side, a button that did nothing: the wait now ends on the press, and the question already in flight is dropped rather than finished for nobody. When a press genuinely lands on nothing — the run had already ended, or the server was restarted under it — the card says which of those it was, instead of leaving the admin to press again harder. **A run the server was restarted under with nobody pressing anything gets the same honesty.** A deploy restarts the server, and so, on a development machine, does saving any source file; the run dies with it. The card used to go back to the run before's verdict as if nothing had been tried; it now says the last sync stopped when the server was restarted under it and how far it had got — *412 of 1,083 done: 37 created, 12 updated* — that what it wrote stands, that starting it again goes over everything with the answers the source already gave kept, and how many of the objects it moved are not in their regions yet — *37 objects this source moved are not in their regions yet. The next sync places them and clears this note; Region Assignment places them sooner.* Placing them was the run's last step, so a restart used to leave them in no region for good: their points do not move again, so no later run would have placed them. Now the run's own record names them and the next sync takes them over. A restart can also land in that last step, on a run that had otherwise finished: the card says it went through all its objects but the server was restarted while it was placing what it moved, and how many wait, and the run reads Partial rather than Success until the next sync has placed them. A preview, and a run stopped while still collecting from the source, are told in their own words, and the sync history calls such a run what it is rather than a run from before change records were kept. A card following a run keeps asking through the seconds the server is away, and says it has lost touch only after about two minutes of silence; a run another server is running shows its progress from that run's own record, with its Cancel reading *Running on another server*. And a run that stopped leaves its own last words on the card — *the picture repair failed: Wikidata did not answer, so nothing was changed — try again later*, or that it was cancelled — where before a repair's failure vanished in the tick it arrived and the chip fell back to the previous sync's verdict, as if nothing had been tried. There is no standing "assignment needed" warning any more, because a run that succeeds leaves no such question open. The Region Assignment panel remains for two cases a sync cannot settle by itself. The ordinary one is that **regions changed** — their boundaries, or where they sit in the tree — so every location has to be tested against them again; nothing does that on its own yet. The other is that **the placement at the end of a run failed**, or a restart stopped it — the run then shows as Partial rather than Success, with the reason in its log entry, because the catalogue is correct while the region assignments for what it moved are not. The next sync of that source places those objects again by itself; pressing the button is for not waiting until then. It rebuilds a whole world view — which is exactly why a sync does not use it — and it does so in one go: regions keep the assignments they had until it finishes, so nobody browsing sees them empty while it runs, and a stop, a failure or a server restart part-way leaves them exactly as they were. When the server is restarted under it the panel says so — nothing it did was kept, the assignments from before stand, start it again — rather than going quiet as if it had finished
- **Discover opens on something to browse** — an admin's map opens on the administrative divisions, which have no regions to list, so Discover opens on the first world view that has them instead of an empty tree under a blank picker; the map then shows that world view too, since the two keep one place
- **Manage world views** — full CRUD on regional hierarchies, assign divisions to regions, compute geometries. Computing a whole world view's geometry, which takes over an hour for the Administrative one, runs in the background worker that also builds the public dumps: a deploy or a restart of the server no longer stops it part-way, the admin sees it waiting, running region by region and how it ended, and a computation the worker itself was stopped in says so and is started again to finish what is left: *Compute All* computes only the regions still missing their outline and the ones above them, unless the admin ticks *Recompute every region*. When splitting GADM divisions into children, existing child regions (e.g., from a WorldView import) appear as assignment targets — admins manually assign which GADM geometry goes to which existing region via dropdowns. The "Create Custom Subregions" dialog also pre-populates target groups from existing child regions, letting admins drag divisions into imported regions. The Map View tab highlights the image overlay button when a region map is available — one click loads the map from Wikimedia Commons as a reference image, viewable either as a map overlay or side-by-side with the division map for precise geometry alignment. The calibrated map and the groups being sorted belong to the region, not to the browser they were made in: every admin, on any machine, opens the region on the same map and the same groups, saved as they change, and an admin can remove the map; a map an earlier session left only in one browser is offered once, to keep for everyone or discard, and groups it left are taken over where the region has none. The dialog can be opened for any region — when a region has child regions but no direct divisions, descendant geometries are shown as a read-only context layer (color-coded by group) while only direct members remain interactive. A region shown by its territorial extent rather than its coastline follows the box that says so: ticking “use hull” now redraws the region on the main map at once, where before the overview kept the outline traced from its real coastline while the island layer switched to the hull immediately — one region drawn as two different shapes, the stale one served from cache on top of it. And an edit reaches everyone's map, not only the editing admin's: every save that changes what the map draws — a drawn boundary, a reset, a hull, a rename, a new colour, a move, a region created or deleted, here or in an import's review — changes the address its tiles are fetched from. Before, only a geometry computation did, so a boundary saved by hand stayed the old shape on every other visitor's map, served from the map server's cache, until the next computation happened to run. And an edit lands whole or not at all: moving a region, deleting one with its branch, flattening or expanding it, in the editor or in an import's review, is one step that either happens entirely or leaves the tree as it was. Before, a failure or a second admin's edit in the middle could leave children moved and the region still there, or a region moved without the continents it left and joined being marked for recomputing. When a branch changes while an import review is flattening it, the flatten is refused and asks to be run again, rather than absorbing a branch nobody looked at. Every member is drawn, however small: the outlines are simplified for the screen in a way that cannot delete one, where a division under about half a kilometre across used to be simply absent from the map its region was being edited on — 3,366 of GADM's divisions are that small, Rwandan cells and Philippine barangays among them. The hull editor opens on a region's own tuned settings, or says it could not load them and offers to retry. Until they are loaded, nothing can be previewed or saved, so a dropped connection cannot quietly put the defaults over a hull someone tuned. The hull editor shows a saved hull only where the region has one: for a region without, its dashed hull layer stays empty until a preview is drawn, where before it traced the region's own outline under the name of a saved hull. A "Move to parent" tool lets admins click individual divisions to push them up to the parent region, and an "All to parent" batch action moves all unassigned divisions at once. A cut part stays the part it is through a reorganisation: expanding a region into subregions gives each part its own subregion under its own name, and deleting a region while keeping its children hands its members to the parent as they were, so Russia west of the Urals does not grow back into the whole of Russia. Several search results can be staged and made one region in a single step; the dialog suggests the name the staged names share — "Kazakhstan" for the two rows GADM keeps for it — and then leaves the field to the admin: the suggestion can be replaced or cleared, and a cleared field stays cleared. **Manual Cluster Paint Editor** — when the CV auto-match pipeline produces incorrect cluster boundaries, admins can switch to a vector-border paint editor with paint bucket (flood fill), border eraser, and polyline drawing tools to manually correct cluster assignments. Borders are extracted from the cluster output as SVG vector paths (via OpenCV findContours) and rendered as a scalable overlay with smooth curves; the eraser splits paths at the cursor, and the line tool draws new polylines with automatic endpoint snapping. Flood fill rasterizes the SVG on demand and stops at the vector borders. The editor overlays a transparent color canvas on the map and submits the result to replace automated pixel labels before ICP alignment proceeds
- **Describe a world view without hitting a wall** — every description field — the create dialog, the settings dialog, and the inline editor in the world view header — carries a live character counter and stops at 1,000 characters, the length the system keeps — so the limit shows up while the description is being typed, rather than as a save that fails for no stated reason. A save the system still refuses — in the create, settings or delete dialog, in the header's inline editor, or in the editor's region dialog — says why in the place it was made, under the field it names ("Too big: expected string to have <=255 characters" under the name), and the dialog or inline editor stays open with it rather than closing as if the change had landed. The settings and region dialogs send only what changed, so renaming a region writes its name alone, and cannot put back a parent or colour another admin changed while the dialog was open. A description or a source can be taken away as well as written: emptying either and saving leaves the world view without one, where the old text used to come back
- **Publish or hide world views** — toggle "Visible to everyone" per world view in its settings dialog (custom world views only — the picker offers no Settings entry for the default world view, so its flag is reachable only through the API); a hidden one is unlisted and unreadable through the API to everyone else (its map tiles aren't yet scoped by the same visibility check), and newly imported world views start hidden until an admin publishes them.
- **Import WorldView** — one panel with a source selector: Wikivoyage, a JSON file, or the administrative base layer. Choosing **Wikivoyage** and clicking "Fetch from Wikivoyage" automatically extracts the full Wikivoyage region hierarchy from the kept copy of English Wikivoyage (*Keep English Wikivoyage's articles*), enriches with Wikidata IDs, creates a WorldView, and matches countries to administrative divisions — all from a single button click with live multi-phase progress (extraction → enrichment → import → matching, 20–40 minutes before the spatial pass below, which adds hours: each region the names left unmatched is read politely from Wikivoyage, and tried again on every run). Choosing **JSON file** uploads a pre-generated JSON tree for any other external source. A region's map image in that tree is held to what a picture may be — an absolute http(s) address, refused at upload otherwise — and is drawn only from a host the app trusts for pictures (Wikimedia Commons, where every map imported so far lives), so a map hosted elsewhere is kept but never shown, and the picker offers only maps it can draw. It is never *read* either: the colour match says so at once and stops, naming the map, rather than fetching an address a JSON tree may point anywhere — so a map from another host makes the region one to match by hand, instead of a long wait ending in nothing. Wherever an admin's screen draws a region's map — the picker, the Division Preview, the CV match review, the flatten, simplify, gap and overlap dialogs, and the reference map of Create Subregions — the map's author and licence are shown under it, as under every other picture the product shows: the Algeria map reads "Peter Fitzgerald · CC BY-SA 4.0". A map that fails to load takes its line with it, and a reference map the admin uploaded carries none. A region's shape drawn from a Commons map file — on the coverage comparison, the transfer preview and the Wikivoyage map of the colour match — is credited the same way, by its file and licence: "Shape: Data:North_Sea_Coast_region.map, ODbL-1.0 (Based on OpenStreetMap data)"; one cut from OpenStreetMap reads "Shape: © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL-1.0". A file that declares no licence is not drawn at all. A region's source page — the Wikivoyage article it came from — is held to the same shape at upload, and is offered as a link (the glyph in the review tree, the source-page button in the Custom Subregions map view, "View source page" in the CV match review) only where it is an http(s) address; anything else is named, not linked. Choosing the **administrative base layer** imports the administrative divisions currently loaded into their own world view — one region per division, down to a chosen depth — matched by the same pipeline and landed in the same review as any other import; like any new import, the world view starts hidden. Once its matching completes, the import card offers to draw its outlines straight away, with the progress, how many regions failed and any reason it could not start shown right there, rather than leaving the admin to find the button in the editor. The Wikivoyage form says which month's copy the fetch reads, so two fetches from one copy build the same tree; with no copy kept it says to build one under Public Dumps, and the fetch waits for it. Each source gets a distinct source type (`wikivoyage`, `imported`, or `base_layer`) shown in the existing world views list. Matching policy follows the source rather than being asked for: Wikivoyage and JSON-file imports get "Country-based", which looks for country names anywhere in the tree, and the administrative base layer gets "Hierarchical", which walks down the division tree alongside the imported one — that is what lets it resolve every region of a mirror instead of the 62% the country-based route reached. Its matching shows its progress region by region ("Matching regions: N/M"), as a country-based import shows it country by country. A JSON-file import can also be set to "None" to skip auto-matching entirely and assign everything by hand. Review/accept matches through a dedicated admin interface with a hierarchical tree view. The tree view uses role-based rendering: containers show "X/Y matched" summaries, countries show status chips with GADM names, and `children_matched` countries expand to show subdivision assignments. Sub-continental groupings (Melanesia, Polynesia, etc.) can be handled via a "Handle as sub-continental" button that clears the parent's match and re-runs country-level matching on each child independently. Division assignments made via the WorldView Editor are automatically reflected back in the match review interface (bidirectional sync). A "Re-match All" button resets and re-runs the matcher after improvements, under the same policy the source implies — the dialog is a plain confirmation and offers no policy choice — it deletes every division assignment for the world view first, including ones accepted by hand, so hand-work belongs after a re-match rather than before it. Because each run discards the previous result and its spatial pass reads every region the names left unmatched again, the button is limited to five presses a minute and answers "too many requests" beyond that. A running re-match can be stopped from its progress notice: stopped while it matches by name, the world view stays as the reset left it, every region unmatched, and the notice says so; stopped during the spatial pass, it keeps the matches and suggestions already made and says where the pass stopped. "Close Review" finalizes the import (disabled until every region is matched and the coverage check reports no active uncovered administrative divisions). The coverage check opens at once even on a large import: it brings each uncovered division with the level of divisions directly under it, and a deeper level is read the moment the reviewer expands it, where it used to bring every uncovered division's whole tree up front — some eight megabytes for a Wikivoyage import, most of it under entries nobody opened. Previewing a smart flatten changes nothing: it shows the shape the flatten would give, with the matches it would make for the region's subregions, and only confirming the flatten uses them, so a closed preview or a "Cannot flatten" leaves the tree as it was, and *Undo* restores the subregions as they were before the flatten, with none of its matches on them. **Simplify Hierarchy** — collapse fully-covered subtrees (where every GADM child of a parent is already a member) up into the parent region with one click, useful when GADM has more granularity than the world view needs. **Smart Simplify** — detects when a GADM parent's children are split across multiple sibling regions and proposes consolidating them into one, showing a side-by-side map view of current vs proposed assignments so the admin can apply or skip each move. Spatial anomalies (exclaves and disconnected fragments) are highlighted in the same dialog with Accept/Skip actions. **After the name matcher, both spatial matches also run on their own**: every leaf the names left unmatched that carries a Wikidata id gets Geoshape Match, Point Match where no geoshape exists, and the same IoU scoring, which auto-accepts a perfect fit. So Kabardino-Balkaria, whose name GADM does not share, arrives in review with its covering divisions suggested instead of nothing. The run's completion message says how many such leaves got suggestions and how many were accepted, and cancelling during this pass keeps the name matches already made. **Geoshape Match** — uses IoU scoring against a shape the import holds for the Wikivoyage region to find the best-covering set of GADM divisions. A region that is a grouping no boundary encloses, such as the Middle East or West Africa, is matched against the union of its subregions' shapes when every subregion has one. The shapes come from the catalogue's own copy of OpenStreetMap: every weekly update of the copy keeps the outline of each region an import names, wherever an OpenStreetMap boundary or area carries the region's Wikidata item, so a coast or a peninsula that is no administrative unit is matched by its own outline too. It also keeps the outline of each district a region's Wikivoyage map is drawn from, such as the Ammerland and Aurich districts on the Lower Saxony map, so the match can draw that map. A region named for the first time gets its outline from the next update, and a region OpenStreetMap draws no area for says it has no shape and is matched by its Wikivoyage markers. Wherever such an outline is drawn, "© OpenStreetMap contributors" is shown under it. The import fetches no shapes from Wikimedia's map service, whose terms do not let a third-party service use it. The Division Preview Dialog draws no region shape: it shows the region map image or the marker points. **Point Match** — for regions without any geoshape, extracts Wikivoyage marker coordinates and finds GADM divisions that contain those points. Below a matched parent it suggests the largest of them under that parent. With no matched parent it suggests the smallest one holding every point, so a point on Tahrir Square suggests Cairo's Qasr an-Nil district, not Africa and Egypt beside it. Marker locations are stored for preview in the Division Preview Dialog as orange circle markers on the left map panel. The Division Preview Dialog adds a fourth mode: when a region has marker points but no geoshape or region map image, it shows those points as orange dots on the left-side map so the admin can visually verify the match before accepting. **Scope Fallback** — when Geoshape or Point Match returns no candidates (e.g. island groups whose centroid falls outside GADM polygons), a "Try wider: `<scope>`" link appears inline; clicking it retries the search at the next ancestor level (country → continent → world) without auto-triggering. **Conflict Detection and Accept-With-Transfer** — when a matched GADM division is already assigned to a sibling region, a warning chip ("from Mexico (split Baja California Sur)") marks the suggestion as a conflict. Clicking the map icon or Accept opens a Transfer Preview Dialog showing a three-layer map: the donor region's full geometry in red, the divisions that would move in orange, and the target region outline in dashed blue. The admin clicks "Accept Transfer" to atomically move the divisions and recompute both regions' geometries. **Deciding several suggestions at once** — selecting some of a region's suggestions lets the admin accept them and reject the rest, or reject just them, in one step that lands whole or not at all. **Split Deeper** — from the Division Preview Dialog, a matched division can be opened one level down, showing its children with the article's marker points where a deeper shape does not exist. **Vision Match** — for a region with a map image, a vision model reads the map and says which of the candidate divisions it shows, which it does not, and which it cannot tell. **AI Extraction Interview** — during Wikivoyage extraction, when the AI is uncertain (e.g. should a region be split into subregions?), it pauses and asks the admin one structured question with a recommended option; the admin's answer is final for that page (the page is not re-asked), and the world view is imported with the regions it gave the page: an answer that merges three subregions into two imports two. When the answer generalizes, it also produces a soft rule that informs future extractions. **AI Review Children** — on any region whose Wikivoyage source URL is set, an AI Review button (sparkle icon) audits the region's current child set against its Wikivoyage article. The AI reads the live Wikivoyage wikitext, compares it to existing child regions, and produces a grouped action list: regions to **Add** (missing children from the article), regions to **Remove** (children that no longer appear), and regions to **Rename** (children whose names differ). Each action shows the reasoning and, for Add/Rename actions, a verified Wikivoyage URL. Add and Rename actions are pre-selected by default; Remove actions (destructive) are opt-in. The admin reviews the grouped dialog, checks or unchecks individual items, then clicks Apply to execute all selected actions at once. After all actions complete, the tree refreshes automatically.
- **CV Settings** — a dedicated admin panel to switch the CV color-match pipeline between JavaScript (OpenCV.js WASM) and Python (FastAPI microservice using OpenCV + scikit-image). The setting persists to the database; the Python path is preferred when the service is healthy, with automatic fallback to JavaScript if the service is unreachable.
- **Manage curators** — promote users to curator role, assign scopes (region, source, or global), revoke assignments, and read what one curator has done: their trail opens beside them, each act named in the same words the object's own history uses — “kept out”, “put back”, “took the source’s” — with the recorded payload underneath for the reading that is an audit rather than a story. Administrators appear here too, with a non-revocable **Global, by role** scope and the same way to open their activity, even when nobody has assigned them a scope
- **Read the week's curation in one place** — *Curation Activity* lists what every curator did to the catalogue, newest first: what was published, kept out, edited or merged, on which place, in which region, by whom and when. The catalogue's own merges are there too, signed by the catalogue rather than a person. An admin narrows the feed by action, by curator (an administrator among them, and someone whose curator scope has since been taken away), by region — Japan keeps the acts on Yasukuni Shrine and Saihō-ji alike, wherever in Japan they sit — and by days, instead of opening one curator's trail after another
- **Region assignment** — run the spatial assignment algorithm that maps experiences to regions based on their coordinates
- **Ask the catalogue what it holds** — a *Catalogue Checks* screen putting a set of claims to the live database: a place is not stored twice, an object offered to visitors has somewhere to go, a picture on a page names its photographer, a museum its kind has turned away is not still badged as a must-see, a famous work is not left on no card a visitor can reach — the Venus of Willendorf, whose museum in Vienna is no art museum, was — and — since defects in the map keep being found by someone looking at it and by nothing else — where the map thinks a region sits is where it is: a region framed as the whole world while its outline is not, an anchor further than five hundred kilometres from the region it belongs to, a continent drawn as a fraction of its countries, a country with no outline at all, a district standing for the leftover its named towns do not cover while those towns are counted nowhere, and a coarse copy of an outline drawing holes the country itself does not have, or leaving out islands it does. It also asks the opposite question: what travellers are sent to that a kind does not offer although its own rule calls it well known — the Frida Kahlo Museum in Mexico City, Ollantaytambo above the Sacred Valley — read off the lists of what guides recommend in each surveyed region, with how much of what is expected each kind already offers said beside them, so a run that loses a place every guide names shows up as a number that grew. A gap somebody has already judged — a museum that waits for a tier the product does not run yet, a chapel a reader finds under another kind, a commercial gallery no museum kind owes — is counted by its cause beside the rows and not listed again, so what the screen leads with is only what nobody has answered for. And the reverse: what a kind offers in a surveyed region that no guide sends anyone to — a stump of brick in the Forum, an aqueduct whose course runs underground — is put to a person least known first, and a place they read as not worth the visit stays on the screen until the kind's rule or a curator turns it away, so a catalogue padded with junk shows up as plainly as one that misses the Colosseum. Each says what it found in the words of the thing — "Aldabra Atoll (185): in Seychelles, and in no region" — rather than as a rule id and two numbers, and the ones that need a person are what the page leads with.

  **A number the admin has accepted is quiet.** A catalogue built from four sources over years holds rows nobody is proud of, and a screen that showed every one of them in red every morning is a screen nobody opens twice: 28 objects sit in no region at all today, mostly islands and reefs a boundary set built from land has nowhere to put. So accepting a number records it — with who accepted it and when — and from then on that check reads as debt being carried. What raises attention instead is the number **growing**, which means something is writing those rows now rather than a backlog somebody already knows about; a number that has fallen says so too, so work that fixed something is visible. A check nobody has answered for yet says exactly that, and asks to be answered rather than passing quietly.

  Accepting is one check at a time, deliberately: a single button that accepted everything would make the morning somebody answers for a newly added rule also the morning a real regression got recorded as normal. And the count of visits recorded against a place no longer shown offers no button at all — those records are legitimate, a traveller who stood somewhere stood there, so the number is one to watch rather than a debt to answer for
- **Place what landed just outside every region** — a place a few metres outside every boundary belongs to no region: the Island of Gorée 14 m off Senegal's coastline, the Sea organ on the Zadar quay, Dublin's centre, which Ireland's outline does not reach. *Catalogue Checks* lists every place a reader is offered that no region holds, nearest first, with the nearest region and the next and how far each is. Gorée is 14 m from Dakar with nothing else within 28 km, an easy call. Ha Long Bay is 6 km from Hải Phòng and 10 km from Quảng Ninh, a real question. Nothing is placed without a curator: adding the place to the right region by hand settles it. A place in the open ocean, more than 30 km from any region, gets no suggestion.
- **Say which side of a border a place is on** — a region's outline is only as exact as the boundary data drawing it, and some internal borders are drawn with a point every kilometre or more, so a place near one can land in the neighbouring region: Geghard and Garni under Ararat though they stand in Kotayk, the Umayyad Mosque outside Damascus, the Valley of the Kings outside Luxor. *Catalogue Checks* lists every place within a kilometre of another region whose own Wikidata entry does not confirm the side it was put on, with what that entry says — the neighbour, both, neither, nothing — and the distance. Nothing is moved on Wikidata's word, since it is wrong sometimes too (it would move Old St. Peter's into Lazio): a curator decides. Moving the place to the right region, or adding it by hand to the region it is in, settles it, and it is not asked about again.
- **Monitor system health** — sync logs, error tracking, database status

## The Experience System

Experiences are the atomic units of travel engagement — anything a user can discover, learn about, or do in connection with a region. A visitor browses them by **kind of place** — what they would call the thing in front of them — and each kind is its own list, its own pin colour and its own count: a person who collects cathedrals finds Cologne Cathedral among cathedrals, a person who collects World Heritage finds it there, and it is one place with one visit either way; a person who has been inside the Neues Museum has been on Museum Island, because a place that stands inside another counts as a visit to the whole — never the other way round, and never across a serial World Heritage site, where one of its locations marks that location and not the site (ADR-0046); a person who loves history museums and not art museums gets two lists, not one list with a filter (ADR-0045). Where the data comes from is not something a visitor sees. The Iconic badge the lists below give a place the whole world knows is recorded on the place but not yet shown to a visitor on it — a visitor sees it on a museum's works and finds only — so today a place the world knows and one its region knows look alike (#2177). World Heritage Sites, Art museums, Public art and Monuments & memorials are on the map. Places of worship, Archaeology, Towers, Bridges & engineering marvels and Squares & streets have nothing published yet: every kind seeded since Places of worship arrives for a curator to read first, and each of the five is seen once a curator has published what its runs brought. Each is filled by one source so far: World Heritage Sites (~1,250, from UNESCO), Art museums (~100 — filled today only by the museums that hold a world-famous work, so the list is short and every entry carries the Iconic badge; a work fewer Wikipedias write about but the world reads as much as the famous ones — the *Veiled Christ*, read about 270,000 times a year — can bring in its museum, the Cappella Sansevero, and a church can be brought in the same way, but only for a source whose arrivals a curator reads first, with the work and its year of reading named on the card and the question whether a visitor can see it there now; and its header reads "Art Museums" — the kind's own name since #818, not the works-first source's; regional art museums without one, and archaeology and history museums as kinds of their own, arrive with their own syncs; a museum is listed under the name on its door — the Palazzo Pitti rather than the Galleria Palatina inside it, the Petit Palais rather than the city's fine-arts collection it houses, the Brooklyn Museum rather than the centre that shows *The Dinner Party* — while a museum that merely occupies a building keeps its own name, as the Galleria Borghese does in its villa; and a work nobody can see — stolen and never found, lost, destroyed — is not on any museum's card and gets no museum in for it, so the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum is not listed for the two paintings cut from their frames in 1990, while the Colossus of Constantine, whose fragments fill a Capitoline courtyard, stays), and Public art and Monuments & memorials (~90 and ~115, read from one pool — the world's best-known outdoor sculptures, statues, fountains and murals in one list, and its monuments, memorials, arches, columns and obelisks in the other, what Wikipedia knows in 22 languages or more, each carrying the Iconic badge for that; beside them, the walls, lanes and tunnels a traveller goes to for the street art painted on them, each taken whole as one place since its works are repainted while the place stays — the Lennon Wall in Prague, Hosier Lane in Melbourne, Beco do Batman in São Paulo, the Freedom Tunnel in New York, the East Side Gallery in Berlin — found through what English Wikipedia and Wikidata file under street art and graffiti or call a wall of graffiti paintings, and let in on two of three signs that travellers go there: an Atlas Obscura entry, a travel guide naming the place, and 10,000 readers a year; a wall that was whitewashed and torn down, 5 Pointz, is not on the list; a wall's card lists the murals painted on it that Wikidata links to it and has a photograph of, the best known first and none left out for being little known — the East Side Gallery's seven, Dmitri Vrubel's *My God, Help Me to Survive This Deadly Love* first and the only one marked must-see, since the others are written about in one or two languages — each with its photograph's credit; and a courtyard known only in its own language, Haus Schwarzenberg in Berlin, waits for the regional tier; until #1807 they were one list, Public Art & Monuments, and a curator's earlier answers on a place carry over to both; a cathedral or a museum that Wikidata also calls a monument is not among them, nor a work that hangs inside a church or a museum, nor one that a museum's or a place of worship's collection holds when nothing says where it stands, nor a sculpture that stands on the Moon — while a memorial with a museum inside it is, because a traveller stands in front of Tsitsernakaberd's flame or the 9/11 pools whether or not there is a museum below, and so is a monument the museum in its base administers, like the Pakistan Monument; the rule that says so names its reason on the review page, where a curator can overrule it; the monuments a region knows and the world's line does not reach are the regional tier's, as much the kind's as these and found later through regional sources, and they will carry no badge without being worth less for it), and Places of worship (~1,080 — its first live run is still pending, so the rows arrive gated and none of it is on the map yet; the cathedrals, churches, chapels, monasteries, mosques, temples, shrines and synagogues the world knows, each admitted either because the building itself is famous or because it holds a work that is: St Peter's is in for both, and the Church of Santo Tomé is in for *The Burial of the Count of Orgaz* alone; the works, relics, tombs and crypts inside are the place's treasures, so the *Pietà* is St Peter's; a tower is not, because nobody enters a church to see its bell tower, so the Leaning Tower and the Minaret of Jam are Towers'; the source is one anybody can edit, so its rows will arrive for a curator to read first, same as any other row from it), and Archaeology (the dig you stand in and the museum that shows what came out of it, in one list, because a traveller planning Egypt wants Saqqara and the Egyptian Museum together; the museums had no list of any kind until now — the art-museum rule rightly turns away an institution whose famous holdings were dug up rather than painted, and the British Museum by name, so a visitor browsing museums in Egypt, Mexico, Turkey or Greece found no archaeology museum at all and one in London got art museums without the British Museum; a museum is admitted here for what it *is* — what English Wikipedia and Wikidata say the place is about — and never for one famous object it happens to own, so the Uffizi is not an archaeology museum for owning the Venus de' Medici and nobody collecting archaeology museums is sent to it, while an art museum with a real antiquities department, the Hermitage or the Vatican Museums, arrives with the question written on it for a curator to answer, and one whose antiquities gallery is a gallery among many, such as the Cleveland Museum of Art, is turned away with that reason rather than asked about. A museum the catalogue's sources never call archaeological is still found where travellers' own map does: the Altes Museum in Berlin, home of the Antikensammlung, is marked an archaeological museum on OpenStreetMap, and that is how it gets in — and so does a museum of no archaeological nature holding a famous find that no other list shows, the Natural History Museum in Vienna with the Venus of Willendorf or the Viking Ship Museum in Oslo with the Oseberg ship, since a curator's yes is the only way such a find reaches a card before the catalogue has a kind for its museum, while the Uffizi's ancient statue is already on the Uffizi's art-museum card and asks nothing; what such a museum holds is listed as its finds, each saying where it was dug up, since the Rosetta Stone is a British Museum object and a Fort Julien one, the fort at Rashid where it was found — and it reads the same wherever the museum is opened, on the map and in Discover alike, because one museum described two ways is two claims about one place; a dig's card lists what was dug up there and where a traveller can see it, and its row in the list says how many before the card is opened — Mycenae reads "Mask of Agamemnon, shown at the National Archaeological Museum of Athens", the Acropolis names the British Museum and the Acropolis Museum both for the Parthenon Frieze — and the museum's name is a way to its card wherever the world view on screen places it; the find's own row answers back, so "found at Mycenae" on the Athens museum's list opens Mycenae, while a find dug up in a city, a region or a place no dig on the list names keeps the words alone; and what a museum holds is read from the museum's own side as well as by the kinds of thing a find can be, so the Pergamon Museum lists the Ishtar Gate and the Pergamon Altar — the two things it is visited for, which no list of find classes names — beside the stelae a class brought in, and a museum is marked must-see for them; the Iconic badge is not given for belonging here, as it is in the museum and monument lists, but for holding a find the world knows — the British Museum wears it for the Rosetta Stone, while the Bardo in Tunis is in the list in full standing and wears none, because a museum earns its place by what it is about and the badge by what is inside it; a museum that belongs to two kinds, the Louvre among them, is two rows and two pins on the map until the two become one place, and once it is, it has one pin, drawn as a disc split into one slice per kind it is shown in — on the world map too, before any region is chosen — and the card that comes up under the pointer names every kind it belongs to; a click on that pin opens its card above the list's kinds, naming each of them, and the card lists the place's works once — the Capitoline Wolf is one row, not one per kind — each marked with the kinds that hold it, under a chip per kind that says how many works it would add, the kind the card was opened from chosen and every kind from the pin, while its row in each kind's list says it is the one selected, and a click on one of those rows moves the card into that kind, and the region's list shows it under each of its kinds, and the Discover list and the world map of either kind find it, each showing it in that kind's colour and type; and the digs are here too: an archaeological site a traveller stands in — Pompeii, Troy, Saqqara, Angkor — admitted for being one of the world's, or, where fewer Wikipedias write about it, for being read as much as the sites that are — the Roman Baths in Bath and Largo di Torre Argentina in Rome, read some 175,000 and 130,000 times a year, and vouched for as a place people go to by at least two of a travel guide, photographs taken there, Wikidata's record of a heritage listing, opening hours, visitors or a website, and the map, since being read about is not being visited: a place read as much with nothing left to see, Denver's old Stapleton airport or the drowned town of Rungholt, stays out; these arrive for a curator to read first with that year of reading named on the card and the question whether visitors go to see it, with the shape of the ground it covers drawn on the map where OpenStreetMap has mapped it, and the card saying how much ground that is — 5.7 hectares at Troy, 11.5 at Machu Picchu, 774 square kilometres over the Nazca lines — with the credit the map asks for beside it; a place that is a living city rather than a ruin is kept out and says which, so Athens, Cairo and Asyut are not on the list while Saqqara and Bagan are, and a shipwreck is not a place anybody stands in; a dig the encyclopedia files as a cave, a mountain or a city — Ajanta, Nemrut, Jerash — is on the list all the same when the map itself marks an archaeological site there, and its card says so, while the map's word alone puts no town whose ruins a mapper linked to it, no castle in ruins, no building that no longer exists, and no building still in use on the list — a cathedral whose crypt a mapper tagged as a dig, Reims or Palermo, or a temple where people still worship, Lingaraja, stays out because the map itself says it is used today, unless English Wikipedia counts it among its archaeological sites; no archaeology place exists yet, because no run has written one, and a list called Archaeology with no Pompeii in it would be a claim about the world that is not true, so the first live run, now that both halves are built, is the maintainer's next step — and even then the kind stays out of sight until a curator has read its arrivals and published them, since every row from a source anybody can edit arrives gated; the kind is already offered to a curator creating a place by hand, so the first archaeology place anyone sees could be one a curator made), and Towers (~110, gated on arrival, so none of it is on the map until a curator has published what its first run brings: a tower a traveller climbs or stands under, whatever it was built for — the Leaning Tower of Pisa and the Giralda, Big Ben, the Qutb Minar and the Minaret of Jam, the Giant Wild Goose Pagoda, Belém Tower, the Eiffel Tower and the CN Tower — filed as a bell tower, a minaret, a pagoda, a clock tower, an observation tower, a fortified tower or a tower; a tower Wikipedia is written about in many languages but nobody reads is not one the world knows, so a tower enters on both counts, 15 Wikipedia editions and 25,000 readers a year, and a pagoda bots wrote 37 editions of and under 3,000 people read stays out; a church with a tower is a place of worship and not a tower, while a bell tower with a chapel inside, Ivan the Great, is both; a lighthouse, a city gate's tower and a skyscraper wait for kinds of their own), and Bridges & engineering marvels (~340, gated on arrival: a bridge, an aqueduct, a dam or a tunnel a traveller goes to or through for itself — the Golden Gate Bridge and Tower Bridge, the Charles Bridge and the Ponte Vecchio, the Khaju Bridge in Isfahan, the Pont du Gard, the Hoover Dam, the Channel Tunnel — read about in at least 15 Wikipedias and by 50,000 people a year, or by 10,000 where a travel guide names the work, because the readers of a collapsed viaduct or an expressway bridge are no reason to go and see it; and the places on a canal where its engineering is seen, each one place a traveller stops at — the Falkirk Wheel, the Caen Hill flight of locks, the Ronquières inclined plane, the Soo Locks, Seattle's Ballard Locks — held to a line of their own, since a lock is written about in few languages however many people stop at it: 3 Wikipedias and 15,000 readers a year, or 4,000 where a travel guide names it; a canal itself is not on the list, since a traveller rarely goes to a canal as a whole and its one pin would sit at one end — the Canal du Midi's in Toulouse — until long objects such as canals, trails and roads are shown as what they are; a bridge that fell or a tunnel not yet bored is not on the list, and a Roman aqueduct that stands only as ruins is Archaeology's), and Squares & streets (~330, gated on arrival: a square, a street, a promenade or a stairway a visitor walks as a sight in itself — Red Square, Piazza Navona, the Grand-Place, Naqsh-e Jahan Square, the Spanish Steps, the Champs-Élysées, the Arbat, Havana's Malecón; read about in at least 15 Wikipedias and by 30,000 people a year, or by 5,000 where a travel guide names the place; the Roman Forum is Archaeology's and the Charles Bridge a bridge, though Wikidata files them as a square and a street). A kind appears only once a sync of its own fills it and a rule says what complete means for it — a list of the few churches other imports happened to bring would have been a claim about the world, and a false one, which is why Places of worship waited for a source and a rule of its own, why Archaeology, whose two doors are now built, stays out of sight until its first live run has put the digs on the list beside the museums and a curator has published them, why Towers waited for a rule that tells a tower from the church or the gate it stands beside, why Bridges waited for a line a travel guide checks, and why Squares & streets waited for one that does not lose the Malecón because no guide names it. Plans to expand into books, films, food, festivals, wildlife, and many more kinds.

Users browse experiences in two views: **Map mode** (select a region, see grouped experiences below) and **Discover mode** (tree-based navigation with map + card list). In both views, a kind whose places come in more than one type can be narrowed to one: Italy's World Heritage Sites to their natural ones, a city's places of worship to its mosques — chips under the kind's heading in Discover, under an open kind's group in Map mode, each with how many of its places it keeps, and the map's pins follow the choice. A place's card links out to what a traveller reads next: its Wikipedia article, its own official website for opening hours and tickets, and, for a World Heritage site, UNESCO's page about why it is inscribed, as a link of its own beside the official site rather than in its place. Multi-location experiences (e.g. serial UNESCO nominations) show in-region locations first, with out-of-region locations collapsed behind a "Show N more" toggle and labeled with their region path (common prefix stripped for brevity). Each location is called by its own name — or, where the source left it unnamed, by the name of the place it is, so Etchmiadzin Cathedral reads as the cathedral in its site's list rather than as a pair of coordinates; where the source named it only "The", as it does two of Tarraco's, by its Wikipedia article, so the Roman villa of Centcelles reads as itself; else by the reference the source gives it, so the Via Appia's parts read "1708-003" rather than "Location" over and over — and its full name, object included ("Aalto Works — Villa Mairea, Pori (1752-013)"), is copied from its row in one click, ready to paste into a search or a note. A part shows its own photograph where Wikidata has one — the pile dwelling at Riesi its own lake shore rather than the whole site's picture — credited to whoever took it; a part with none shows the site's picture and says, beside the credit, that it is the whole site's. A part that had no photograph of its own gets one as soon as the catalogue finds it; a different photograph for a part that already shows one waits for a curator, like any change to what visitors see. A curator who knows better can give a part another photograph or rewrite what it says from the object's own screen, and the catalogue then leaves their choice alone. A part also has a card of its own: a click on its row or its pin opens it in the object's place — its photograph and who took it, its name and number, what Wikidata says it is, links to its Wikidata page, the site's page at UNESCO and its official website, and whether you have been there — with a way back to the site and steps through the site's other parts, and the card has an address of its own, so a link to Riesi opens Riesi. A point the list gives the whole site is the site itself, not a part of it: Baalbek and the Historic Centre of Prague no longer list themselves among their own parts, and a link to such a part opens the site. A site of hundreds of parts is browsed rather than scrolled: the parts in the region you are looking at come first, the rest fold by country — or, for a site in one country, by its regions — each group saying how many parts it holds and how many have a photo, with a search by name and a filter for the parts with a photo of their own. Curators maintain content quality through rejection, editing, and manual creation with AI-assisted lookup.

## The Region System

Regions are the geographic building blocks. The system supports multiple ways of dividing the world.

### Administrative Divisions (GADM)

The base layer: official country and sub-country boundaries from the GADM database. Pre-simplified at 5 levels of detail for performant map rendering. Forms a strict hierarchy (country → state → district → ...).

### World Views

Custom hierarchical groupings layered on top of GADM divisions. A world view might group countries into continents, cultural regions, or travel-focused zones.

- **Default world view** — browses the administrative divisions themselves, rather than regions grouped on top of them
- **Base layer mirror** — an optional world view imported one region per administrative division, which is what lets experiences attach to plain administrative geography
- **Custom world views** — user-created groupings. A region can contain whole divisions, specific sub-divisions, or custom-drawn boundaries
- **Computed geometry** — region boundaries are automatically computed from their member divisions, with hull algorithms for scattered geography display
- **Where a place is, in the world you chose** — a place's card and its search result say where it is by the region of the world view you are browsing (a search result the open view does not place names its smallest region in another), never by a country label of its own: which country a ruin of a vanished empire or a church on a disputed hill belongs to is a matter of point of view, and the catalogue does not answer it on a card.
- **Where a place is listed** — under the smallest region whose outline holds it, and under every region above that one: St Peter's is in Vatican City and in Europe, and not in Italy, even though Italy's outline is drawn around the Vatican. A region's outline does not always meet the smaller ones inside it to the metre — GADM draws a coastline a little differently at each level — so a place a few dozen metres off a shore that no smaller region reaches, such as Juno Beach or Delos, is listed under the country and the continent whose outlines do hold it. A place on a border is listed on the side whose outline holds it, and where the two outlines disagree by a few metres that can be the wrong side, which a curator corrects by hand

### Map Rendering

Vector tiles served by Martin (PostGIS-native tile server). The frontend uses MapLibre GL with react-map-gl for:

- Choropleth coloring of regions (visited/unvisited) with clear visual hierarchy — selected, hovered, visited, and default states each have distinct fill and outline intensities
- A continent is drawn whole. Four of the eight top-level regions of the administrative base layer used to draw a fraction of themselves — North America was Mexico, Central America and the Caribbean, with the United States and Greenland not in it at all; selecting Asia framed from Cyprus to Sulawesi and left out Japan and the whole Russian Far East. Nothing looked broken: a continent that draws part of itself looks like a continent. The cause was the order things were computed in — the countries split across two continents got their outlines days after the continents were built from the ones that already had them, and nothing ever went back. Now a region that gains an outline hands the news upward: every region above it that the map builds out of its parts is redrawn on the next pass, and until it is, the map shows nothing there rather than something wrong. A boundary a curator drew by hand is the exception, and stays exactly as drawn — it was never traced from what is inside it, so nothing inside it can make it wrong. And when a run cannot pass the news up — a lock lost to something else working on the same rows — it says so: the curator's completion line turns amber and carries the count, instead of reading a green "Complete!" over a continent it quietly left wrong. A union that simply will not finish is a different answer: the line counts that continent among the skipped, and the next run comes back to it. If something underneath it got through first, the map shows nothing there rather than something wrong; if nothing did, it keeps the outline it had until some run finally finishes — and either way the Catalogue Checks screen names it, so it is never a continent nobody is looking at
- A region keeps the land it holds directly, whatever else it holds. Some regions are made of two kinds of thing at once — a continent that holds a few countries as regions of their own *and* the continent-wide division that covers the rest of it. Cleaning up the seams between the countries used to throw the second kind away, so the region came out as the countries alone: Andorra was stored as its seven parishes and lost 11.8 of its 451 square kilometres to the gaps between them, and North America would have come out without Canada — 9.8 million square kilometres of it, held by nothing but the continent-wide division, because Canada's own outline had not been computed yet. Nothing looked broken there either; a continent missing one country still looks like a continent. Tidying where two neighbours meet is now only that, and never a decision about what the region is made of. Two hundred and thirty-seven regions of the administrative world view are of that mixed kind
- A country is drawn without holes in it. North-eastern Thailand used to show white patches inside the fill around Nakhon Ratchasima, Surin, Khon Kaen and dozens of other district seats — not water, not somebody else's land, just nothing, in a country whose boundaries tile it without a gap. Uruguay was worse: sixteen of its departments drew only the countryside outside their municipios, so all 125 municipios were gaps — Bella Unión, Carmelo, Colonia Valdense and the rest, each named for its town and holding the farmland around it, from four square kilometres to two and a half thousand. The cause was in the reading rather than the drawing. The boundary file leaves some units unnamed, always the same kind: the part of a place its named pieces do not cover, which in Uruguay is the countryside outside any municipio and in Thailand is whichever sub-districts the file does not name. The loader recognised a place by its name, so it handed each nameless one to the place above and stopped there. That place then stood for the leftover alone while its named pieces hung underneath it, counted by nobody — so the province, the country and the continent each drew around a hole where those pieces are. A place with no name is still a place now: it is loaded as itself and everything above it is drawn including it. It is called after the place it sits in, since that is the only name the source offers — and where it ends up standing beside that place's named parts, it says which one it is: under the Thai district of Hang Dong there is now a tambon called Hang Dong and, beside it, "Hang Dong (rest)", the 71 square kilometres the named ones leave over; under the Uruguayan department of Artigas, "Artigas (rest)" is the countryside its three municipios do not cover. What was already loaded wrong is repaired rather than rebuilt, since the pieces above were never wrong about anything except what they were missing
- The map draws the holes the data has, and no others. North-eastern Thailand kept white blobs after the ones above were closed — 66 of them where the data holds 8, the largest over five hundred square kilometres, which at the zoom a visitor reads a continent at is a lake-sized patch of nothing in the middle of Isan. These were not in any boundary. Drawing a continent at every zoom means keeping coarser copies of its outline, and the pass that made them worked on each piece of coastline separately: two pieces that did not touch could be moved until they overlapped, and the tidy-up that follows every such change resolves an overlap by cutting it out — as a hole. So a continent grew holes as it was made smaller, and grew more of them the further out you zoomed. The outlines are now coarsened as one shape rather than piece by piece, so nothing can be moved across anything else, and every copy carries the lakes and enclaves its country has and nothing invented. Small islands were being quietly thrown away by the same pass, and they are back on every copy that is supposed to carry them: Asia was losing 436 of its 26,151 pieces. (The two coarsest copies still leave out pieces below their own scale, deliberately and as described below — that is a choice about what a reader can see at that zoom, not a copy losing what it was given.) Both halves are now watched on the admin panel, so a future upgrade of the mapping library that started dropping islands again would be named rather than noticed years later by somebody looking at Indonesia
- Drill-down navigation (click a region to see sub-regions)
- Ancestor context layers — when drilling into a region hierarchy, all ancestor levels remain faintly visible as dimmed background layers (root siblings, parent siblings, grandparent siblings, etc.), providing full spatial orientation and clickable navigation back to any level
- Experience density on the overview, individual markers up close. Zoomed out, a region reads as a heat surface: where its experiences concentrate, and how strongly, in one glance — a continent shows its centres of gravity instead of a count in a bubble, and zooming in resolves each area into the finer structure inside it. From the marker threshold every experience is a pin of its own. Every experience in a region is represented either way, however many there are, and hovering one in the list rings it on the map
- Region outline persists during exploration mode as a subtle geographic border, giving spatial context alongside experience markers
- A region that straddles the date line is framed like any other. Pick the Far Eastern Federal District and the map settles over the Sea of Okhotsk with Magadan in the middle of the screen; pick Fiji and it settles between Viti Levu and Vanua Levu. Until recently both answered with the whole world at its furthest zoom, the region itself torn in half and pushed against the left and right edges, and the camera pointed at open water on the other side of the planet — Fiji's frame was centred a thousand kilometres off Namibia. It was one wrong measurement, not two: the countries' outlines reach a fraction of a millimetre past the date line, enough for the boundary check to read them as covering every longitude on Earth. Antarctica, which really does, still gets the whole world, because for a continent wrapped around the pole there is no window that would be a frame. Framing works the same way in either world view now, too: a place chosen from the administrative base layer is framed by the same rule as one chosen from a curated hierarchy, where before the base layer had no such rule at all and every dateline region came out as the whole globe. And choosing a place on that base layer no longer downloads its whole outline just to work out where to point the camera — the Far Eastern Federal District's is 17 MB — because where a place is has been worked out once and kept, the way it always was for a curated region. The same rule now frames every other map in the product: an object whose places sit either side of the date line — on the main map and in Discover — is shown as the stretch of ocean between them rather than the whole planet, and the previews a curator or an admin opens while editing a world view frame a Pacific division the way the main map does, where each of them used to measure the shape its own way and get the globe
- Zoomed all the way out, the map draws a coarser rendering of each region than it does up close — detail no one can see at that scale is not fetched, so the world appears in about a second rather than the twenty to twenty-five it took when every coastline was cut down to size on each request. Every region still appears, however small: a region's largest piece of land is kept whatever the scale, so the smallest country on the map is still on it
- The coarse world map is a map, not a mosaic. Borders hold together now: Scandinavia is a coastline rather than a handful of shards, and the Baltic has no white gashes through it — where before, each region was thinned on its own and the two sides of one border came apart by tens of kilometres. Some pairs are still thinned separately, across a country's edge and between the continents at the very top, and there the two sides differ by around a kilometre — a fraction of a pixel at the scales this affects, rather than the tens of kilometres it used to be. (The administrative base layer is not there yet: its own boundaries are still thinned region by region.) What the coarsening leaves out is an offshore island too small to read at the scale in question — a couple of screen pixels or less. It reappears as you zoom in: Bornholm from the third zoom, Gozo from the fifth. A region that *is* an island or a scattering of them always keeps its largest one, so the Isle of Man, Gotland, Saaremaa and Sardinia are drawn at every scale and nothing disappears from the map entirely
- Up close, the outline is the boundary the data actually holds. A region assembled from several divisions used to be *stored* up to about eleven metres coarser than the divisions it was assembled from, while a region that is exactly one division was stored as it came — so two neighbouring regions of the same map were held to two different standards, and a region's own border wandered that far from the borders of the regions drawn inside it. Nothing downstream could put that back: every coarser rendering is made from the stored shape, so the eleven metres travelled up the whole ladder. Regions are now stored at the resolution their sources have, and the thinning happens only where it belongs, in the renderings made for the scales that need it. No exception is left. A region of more than about three hundred thousand points, more than one in four of the regions made of several divisions, continents and countries of a thousand islands among them, used to be assembled from pieces thinned to roughly half a kilometre to save time. Now it is assembled at full resolution too. Europe takes about ten minutes, which is why a world view's geometry is computed in the background worker. A region that does not fit the worker's half-hour bound is left undrawn, and so is the region above it, which would otherwise be drawn without it. The computation's end line counts both as errors, and Catalogue Checks names them among the regions without geometry, rather than drawing them coarser or keeping an outline older than their parts. A region takes on the change the next time its geometry is computed
- Between the world view and a country's own scale — the zooms where you pick out a continent and then a country — the map now arrives twice as fast on the busiest layer and up to nineteen times faster on the continent outlines, because it stopped fetching detail finer than the screen can draw
- A wait for map data never traps the viewer. If tiles have not arrived after a few seconds, or fail outright, the covering "Loading map…" panel steps aside, the map becomes pannable and zoomable, and a small note says some areas are still loading — rather than an opaque screen with no way forward
- The map's own furniture — zoom and compass controls, attribution, popups — is styled by a stylesheet the application ships with, not one fetched from a public CDN as the page loads. A visitor on a network that blocks that CDN, or one who arrives during an outage of it, sees the same map as everyone else rather than controls stripped bare of their styling. It also means the version of those styles can no longer drift away from the version of the map library actually in use
- A browser that cannot draw maps at all says so, once, in plain terms. Maps are drawn with WebGL 2, and some machines do not offer it — hardware acceleration switched off, a graphics driver the browser distrusts, or a browser old enough to have only the first version of WebGL. Where a map would be, such a visitor now reads that it cannot be displayed, why, and that turning on hardware acceleration usually brings it back, along with a line about what still works on that particular screen: the region tree and experience lists never needed WebGL, and a location can still be set by search, coordinates or description instead of by clicking. "Usually" because the remedy fits the first two cases and not the third, and a remedy offered to someone it cannot help is worse than none. Previously this was invisible in the worst way — the main map claimed areas were "still loading" and that panning worked, neither of which was true, and Discover took the whole page down with it

## Design Principles

1. **Reflection over logging** — we help people remember and appreciate where they've been, not passively track their location
2. **Play over data entry** — quizzes, visual maps, and badges make tracking fun rather than tedious
3. **Depth over breadth** — connection levels reward deep engagement with a few places over superficial visits to many
4. **Local expertise** — curators bring regional knowledge; future locals' perspective features amplify authentic voices
5. **Cultural respect** — no ranking cultures or experiences. Cultural relativism guides how we present context
6. **Open data** — built on UNESCO, Wikidata, GADM, and other open sources. Users can export their data
